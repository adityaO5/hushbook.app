# Are Audiobooks Good for ADHD? What the Research Actually Shows

For many people with ADHD, yes: audiobooks make it easier to start and finish books, and the few studies that exist point to less distraction and fatigue. But the evidence is thin, and listening does not get around attention problems on its own. No study shows audiobooks treat ADHD. They work best as one tool among several, used with habits that keep your attention on the story.

The most direct evidence comes from small studies. In a 2002 study of 20 college students with attention disorders, software that read text aloud while showing it on screen helped them read faster, with less distraction, stress and fatigue, though comprehension did not change. A 2021 review of school accommodations for ADHD found that reading aloud was the one accommodation with specific evidence of benefit, from two small randomized trials in children.

## Why reading is hard with ADHD

ADHD affects reading in a few different ways.

- **Mind wandering.** In a 2015 study of 2,708 university students by Seli and colleagues, ADHD symptoms were strongly linked to spontaneous mind wandering, the kind that happens without meaning to (correlations of 0.52 and 0.47 in two samples). A 2019 study of adults with diagnosed ADHD found that mind wandering predicted day-to-day impairment even beyond core ADHD symptoms.
- **Holding the main thread.** In a 2013 study, 27 children with ADHD were matched with 27 children who read words equally well. The children with ADHD fell furthest behind in recalling a passage's central ideas, and working memory explained most of the gap.
- **Overlap with dyslexia.** A 2025 study of more than 19,000 Dutch twins found that a child with ADHD, dyslexia or dyscalculia was two to three times more likely to have a second of these conditions. Most affected children, 77%, had only one. A 2013 review put the overlap between ADHD and learning disabilities in general at about 45%.

So for some people with ADHD the struggle is staying with the text. For others it is decoding the words as well. Audiobooks help with each of these in different ways.

## What the research says about audiobooks and ADHD

There are no large trials of audiobooks for ADHD. Here is what does exist.

- **Reading software for college students.** Hecker and colleagues (2002) followed 20 college students with attention disorders through a semester of reading with software that highlighted text on screen while reading it aloud. The students said it helped them "attend better to their reading" with less distractibility, stress and fatigue, and they read faster. The study found no significant effect on comprehension overall. It had no control group, so treat it as an early signal.
- **Read-aloud tests for children.** Spiel and colleagues ran two small randomized studies, with 36 and 45 children. In both, having test questions read aloud gave children with ADHD a boost that children without ADHD did not get. In the 2019 study, the questions were read by a recording.
- **The review.** Lovett and Nelson reviewed 68 studies of school accommodations for ADHD in 2021. They found that "most accommodations fail to show evidence of benefits that are specific to students with ADHD." The exception was read-aloud accommodations. They also advise using accommodations alongside proven ADHD treatment, not instead of it.
- **If dyslexia is also in the picture.** A 2018 meta-analysis of 22 studies found that text-to-speech and read-aloud tools improved reading comprehension for students with reading disabilities, a small-to-moderate effect. It was not an ADHD study, but it applies to the many people who have both. We cover it in [are audiobooks good for dyslexia](/blog/are-audiobooks-good-for-dyslexia).

## Are audiobooks better than reading for ADHD?

Not automatically. Listening removes the work of decoding and keeps the book moving, which many people with ADHD find easier. But it also removes something that holds attention: your eyes and hands are no longer busy.

In a 2013 study of 211 students in the general population, listening produced more mind wandering and worse memory than reading silently or aloud. A 2003 study of 77 boys found that those with ADHD understood the facts in spoken passages as well as other boys, but had more trouble with inferences and with noticing when they had lost track. In other words, attention problems follow you into audio.

That is why many ADHD listeners do best when they combine listening with something that anchors attention: movement, the text in front of them, or a speed that keeps the story moving. When your mind drifts on audio, the narrator keeps going, and finding your place again is half the battle. ## Tips for listening to audiobooks with ADHD

None of these are tested treatments. They are practical ideas, with the evidence noted where there is some.

### Move while you listen

Walking, tidying or doodling can help some people. In a 2015 study of boys aged 8 to 12, more movement went with better working memory in boys with ADHD, though not better attention. A 2016 study of 44 children found more intense movement went with better performance on a focus task, but only in the ADHD group. Neither study used audiobooks, so treat this as something to try, not a promise.

### Try a faster speed, then check yourself

Many listeners with ADHD say 1x feels too slow and their mind fills the gaps. No study has tested playback speed in ADHD. In the general population, a 2022 UCLA study found comprehension of lecture videos held up to 2x and fell at 2.5x. If you speed up, check that you can still summarize the last chapter. Our guide to [audiobook listening speed](/blog/audiobook-listening-speed) has more.

### Use background noise carefully

A 2024 meta-analysis of 13 studies found white or pink noise gave a small boost to task performance in young people with ADHD and slightly hurt performance in those without. These were lab tasks, not audiobooks, and the effect was small.

### Keep the text in view for hard books

For dense non-fiction or a book with many characters, following the words gives your eyes a job and shows you exactly where you drifted. One caution from the research: a 2025 study of 76 university students found that showing one word at a time in the center of the screen improved comprehension for students with ADHD, while highlighting words one by one in their normal place on the page did not. That study used no audio, so it does not test reading along with an audiobook, but it is a reminder that what helps varies from person to person.

### Short sessions and quick check-ins

Listen in 20 to 30 minute blocks and pause at the end of each chapter to recall what happened. Recalling is one of the best-supported ways to remember anything. We explain the evidence in [how to retain information from audiobooks](/blog/how-to-retain-information-from-audiobooks).

### Rewind without guilt

You will drift. Everyone does. Go back to the last thing you remember instead of pushing on. More fixes for drifting are in [how to focus on audiobooks](/blog/how-to-focus-on-audiobooks).

## Are audiobooks good for kids with ADHD?

They can be a useful part of the mix. The read-aloud test studies were done with children, and audiobooks let a child with ADHD keep up with class novels and books their friends are reading. But they are not a substitute for reading practice, especially if the child also has dyslexia, or for proven ADHD treatment. Talk to your child's school about read-aloud accommodations if reading tests are a struggle. For what the research says about audiobooks and children in general, see [are audiobooks good for kids](/blog/are-audiobooks-good-for-kids).

## What the evidence does not show

- No randomized trial shows audiobooks treat ADHD or reduce its symptoms.
- The direct evidence is small: one 20-person study of college students without a control group, and two small trials of read-aloud tests in children.
- Listening does not bypass attention or working memory problems. Children with ADHD still struggled with inferences in spoken passages.
- No study has tested synced text with audio, faster playback, or listening while moving, specifically in ADHD.
- What helps is individual. Benefits in the studies were small and varied from person to person.
