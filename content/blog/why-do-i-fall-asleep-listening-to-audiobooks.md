# Why Do I Fall Asleep Listening to Audiobooks? And Is It Bad?

Because a calm voice telling a story gives your mind something to follow that is not your own thoughts, at the time of day your body is already primed to sleep. It is extremely common. In a 2026 Swedish survey of 1,136 adults who listen to audiobooks at bedtime, 53% did it every night, and the most common reason they gave was quieting worry and racing thoughts.

It is not bad in itself. No study has found that falling asleep to an audiobook harms sleep, and a small hospital study found the opposite. There are two real catches. You miss the book, because nothing you hear after you drift off goes in. And people who do it every night are more likely to say they cannot fall asleep without it.

Here is what the research says, and how to listen at bedtime without losing your place or your sleep.

## Why audiobooks make you sleepy

### The story takes the place of your worries

The classic bedtime problem is not noise. It is your own head. Sleep researchers call it pre-sleep cognitive activity: replaying the day, planning tomorrow, the thing you said in a meeting.

In a 2002 study in *Behaviour Research and Therapy*, Harvey and Payne gave 41 people with insomnia different instructions for the pre-sleep period. The group told to distract themselves with an interesting, engaging mental image fell asleep faster and reported less frequent and less distressing pre-sleep thoughts than the group given no instructions. The authors' explanation: an engaging task takes up enough "cognitive space" that worries cannot get back in.

A narrated story does the same job without any effort from you. The Uppsala survey found that reducing worry or racing thoughts was the number one reason for bedtime listening, named by 47% of respondents. Falling asleep was second at 31%, and a comforting background, like a lullaby, was third at 17%.

### Listening asks almost nothing of your body

When you read in print, your eyes move and your hands turn pages. When you listen, especially lying down with the lights off, nothing keeps your body involved. A 2013 study found that listening produced more mind wandering than reading silently or aloud, for exactly this reason. We cover it in [how to focus on audiobooks](/blog/how-to-focus-on-audiobooks). At 2pm that drift looks like daydreaming. At 11pm it looks like sleep.

### You have trained yourself

If you have listened in bed every night for a year, the narrator's voice has become a sleep cue, the way a particular pillow or a bedtime routine does. The survey found that daily listeners were almost twice as likely as occasional listeners to report difficulty falling asleep without an audiobook (adjusted prevalence ratio 1.93). The authors are careful to say this is an association, not proof that audiobooks cause reliance. People who struggle to sleep may simply reach for audiobooks more often. But the habit is real, and it works in both directions.

### It is bedtime

The least interesting explanation is often the right one. You are tired, horizontal and in the dark. The book is not making you sleepy. The clock is. The same chapter at breakfast would hold you fine.

## Is it bad to fall asleep listening to audiobooks?

There is no evidence that it harms your sleep, and some early evidence that it helps.

The most direct test is a 2024 study in *Intensive and Critical Care Nursing* by Mermer and Arslan. Fifty-six patients in a Turkish intensive care unit had their sleep measured on two nights. On the second night, the intervention group listened to a recorded story and the control group had standard care. The audiobook group reported significantly better sleep quality, and their pulse and blood pressure were lower. It is a small study in an unusual setting, so treat it as a hint rather than a verdict.

The Uppsala survey adds the everyday picture. Compared with occasional listeners, daily listeners were more likely to say audiobooks helped them fall asleep (aPR 1.32) and improved their sleep overall (aPR 1.73). People with clinical-level trouble falling asleep got slightly less benefit for drifting off, and were slightly more likely to say they needed an audiobook to do it.

The authors' summary is that bedtime audiobooks "appear to be perceived as a context-dependent sleep support strategy." Christian Benedict, the sleep researcher behind the study, put it more plainly to Uppsala University's news service when the project launched: "I don't believe an audiobook is a miracle cure for poor sleep."

So the honest answer is: for most people it is a harmless, possibly helpful habit. Three things are worth watching.

- **Reliance.** If you cannot fall asleep without a narrator, that is the pattern the Swedish researchers flagged. It is worth having a fallback that does not need a phone, like a dull book or a breathing exercise.
- **Mid-night wake-ups.** If the book plays all night, you may surface at 3am to a loud dramatic scene, or to a dead battery and an earbud still in your ear. A sleep timer fixes this.
- **Persistent insomnia.** If you lie awake for an hour most nights with or without a book, an audiobook is a coping tool, not a treatment. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia is the first-line treatment, and a doctor can refer you.

## Does listening to an audiobook while sleeping work?

No. Your sleeping brain does register sound, but it does not follow a plot.

In a 2017 study in *Nature Communications*, Thomas Andrillon and colleagues played patterns of acoustic noise to sleeping people. During REM and light sleep, listeners picked up the patterns and recognised them better after waking. During deep sleep, the same exposure made recognition worse. The brain was not learning the sounds. It was suppressing them.

A 2019 study in *Current Biology* by Marc Züst and colleagues went a step further and played made-up foreign words with their meanings during deep sleep. On waking, people could guess whether a sleep-played word referred to something large or small slightly better than chance, but only when the word had landed on the peak of a slow wave, and they had no conscious memory of hearing it.

That is the ceiling: a faint, implicit trace of a single word under laboratory timing. Nobody has shown a sleeping listener absorbing a chapter. Whatever played after you dozed off is gone. You will have to rewind, or scroll back through the text if your player shows it, which we come to below.

## How to listen at bedtime without losing the book

### Set a sleep timer

This is the single most useful change. Set it to run slightly longer than it usually takes you to fall asleep, so the book stops close to where you stopped. Fifteen to thirty minutes suits most people. If you tend to drift off mid-chapter, end-of-chapter is tidier, because you always know where tomorrow starts. **HushBook**'s sleep timer offers 5, 10, 15, 30, 45 and 60 minutes, end of chapter, or a custom time, and *fades the volume down* over a few seconds before pausing, so you are not jolted by a hard stop.

### Keep a bed book and a day book

Save the thriller for the commute. At bedtime pick something you have already read, a gentle novel, or slow non-fiction. You lose less if you drift off, and you are less likely to lie awake waiting for the next twist. Benedict suggests exciting content might even carry into your dreams, which is the Tetris effect with detectives.

### Turn it down and slow it down

A lower volume and a slightly slower speed, around 0.9x to 1x, make the narration more of a background hum. If you normally listen at 1.5x, the daytime speed will feel urgent in the dark. We have a separate guide to [audiobook listening speed](/blog/audiobook-listening-speed).

## If you want to stay awake instead

Some people want the opposite. They are trying to finish the book and keep losing. The fixes are the same ones that help with wandering attention, just applied earlier in the evening.

- Listen sitting up, with a light on, before you get into bed.
- Give your hands something simple to do: dishes, folding, a walk.
- Put the words on screen. Reading along keeps your eyes busy, and the highlighted word shows exactly where the narrator is if you drift.
- Stop listening to that book in bed. If it is your bed book, your brain has already filed it under sleep.

And if you tried all that and still dozed off, you have learned something cheap and useful: that book is a good one to fall asleep to.
