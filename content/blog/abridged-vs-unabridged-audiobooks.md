# What Is an Unabridged Audiobook? Abridged vs Unabridged Explained

An unabridged audiobook is the complete book, read word for word. An abridged audiobook is a shortened version, where an editor has cut parts of the text before it was recorded.

If you just want the whole book, choose unabridged. That's what most audiobooks are today. But abridged editions still turn up, especially for older titles, and they're easy to buy by mistake. If you like to read along with the text, it matters even more, for a reason we cover below.

## What gets cut in an abridged audiobook?

Usually the things an editor thinks you won't miss: long descriptions, side plots, minor characters, and in non-fiction, examples, references and technical detail. The main story survives. The texture often doesn't.

Abridged versions can be many hours shorter. Libro.fm, an audiobook store, says publishers sometimes make them to cut running time by several hours.

## Why do abridged audiobooks exist at all?

Mostly history. Audiobooks used to come on cassette tapes and then CDs, and every extra tape or disc cost money to make and ship. A 20-hour novel on a box of cassettes was expensive. Cutting it to six hours made it cheaper and easier to sell.

Downloads removed most of that problem. A long book costs about the same to deliver as a short one, so publishers record the full text and abridged editions have become much less common. You'll still find them on older recordings, some celebrity-read titles, and radio-style dramatizations.

## How to tell if an audiobook is unabridged

- **Check the label.** Most stores print "Unabridged" or "Abridged" on the listing, often in the product details or right under the title.
- **Compare the length.** A typical novel runs somewhere around 8 to 15 hours unabridged. If a 600-page book shows a 5-hour runtime, it has almost certainly been cut.
- **Read the description.** Look for words like "condensed", "adapted", "dramatized" or "radio play". A dramatization with a full cast is a different thing again, and it may change the text.
- **Look at the narrator credit.** A single narrator usually means a straight reading. Lots of named performers often means a dramatization.

If both versions exist, the unabridged one is nearly always the longer listing, even when the price is the same.

## Which should you choose?

Unabridged, in almost every case. You get the book the author wrote, and if you're reading for a class or a book club, you're on the same page as everyone else, literally.

Abridged editions make sense in a few situations: a long non-fiction book where you only want the main argument, a classic you want to sample before committing to 40 hours, or a young listener who'd struggle with the full text. Just know what you're getting.

## Why it matters if you read along

Abridgment causes a specific problem if you like to follow the text while you listen. Pair an abridged recording with a full ebook and the two stop matching within a few pages. Whole paragraphs get skipped and you lose your place.

[HushBook](/) avoids this by making the text from the recording itself. It transcribes the audiobook on your phone, then highlights each word as the narrator reads it. Whatever version you have, abridged or unabridged, the words on screen match what you hear, because they came from the same audio. Tap any word to jump to that moment.

It works with audiobook files you can legally import, such as MP3 and M4B, and with free public domain recordings from its built-in LibriVox and Internet Archive store. LibriVox volunteers generally record complete texts, which makes those a good place to start. Two limits: the transcript comes from speech recognition, so unusual names can come out wrong, and HushBook can't import protected files, such as Audible downloads.

If you'd rather use an ebook and an audiobook together, edition matching matters a lot. [Can you read along with Audible?](/blog/can-you-read-along-with-audible) covers how Amazon handles it.
