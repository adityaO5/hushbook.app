# Is LibriVox Safe, Legal and Free? What to Know Before You Download

Yes. LibriVox is a volunteer project, started in 2005, that records books whose copyright has expired and gives the audio away. It has no ads, charges nothing and needs no account to listen. It is legal in the United States, where every book it records is in the public domain. Outside the US, a book can still be under copyright, and LibriVox leaves that check to you.

The recordings are read by people, not AI. The real catches are elsewhere: narration quality varies, the books are old and recorded exactly as published, and some apps and sellers repackage free LibriVox audio.

## Is LibriVox safe?

Yes. LibriVox's About page sets out its principles: it is "a non-commercial, non-profit and ad-free project," it is "powered by volunteers," and it donates its recordings to the public domain. In October 2026 its catalog lists 21,855 works in 49 languages, recorded by 14,947 readers.

What keeps the risk low:

- **No account to listen.** LibriVox's help page explains how to play any chapter in your browser or download it, and none of the steps involves signing in. You only need an account to post on the volunteer forum.
- **Files come from a known host.** The Internet Archive hosts LibriVox's audio. Each book comes as MP3 chapters at 64 or 128 kbps, as a zip of the whole book, and for most books as a single M4B file. If you are not sure which to pick, see [M4B vs MP3](/blog/m4b-vs-mp3).
- **No payment.** LibriVox audiobooks "are free for anyone to listen to," says its homepage. If a site asks you to pay or to install a downloader to get a LibriVox book, you are not on LibriVox.

The place to be careful is unofficial copies. In a September 2026 update, LibriVox wrote: "Our only official outlet is the LibriVox.org web site." Every app that offers LibriVox books is made by someone else. LibriVox welcomes those apps, but each one sets its own ads, prices and privacy rules, so judge the app on its own.

## Is LibriVox legal?

Yes, in the US. LibriVox records only texts that are in the US public domain, and it releases every recording into the public domain as well. In its own words: "All our audio is in the public domain, so you may use it for whatever purpose you wish."

LibriVox's wiki, last edited in August 2026, accepts three kinds of text:

- works published 96 years ago or earlier,
- works by the US government,
- works that Project Gutenberg has cleared as public domain in the US.

The 96-year rule comes from US law. For books published before 1978, copyright lasts at most 95 years from publication and runs to the end of that calendar year (17 U.S.C. sections 304(b) and 305). So in 2026, books published in 1930 or earlier are public domain in the US. LibriVox's older Public Domain page still says "before 1923"; the wiki has the current rule.

Two details catch people out:

- **Translations have their own copyright.** LibriVox's example is Kafka's *The Metamorphosis*: the German original from 1916 is public domain in the US, but David Wyllie's 2002 English translation is not. That is why LibriVox recordings of translated classics usually use old translations.
- **LibriVox plays it safe.** Its wiki says it is "a volunteer organization with no budget to defend copyright challenges," so it takes "a very cautious approach" to what goes in the catalog.

## Is LibriVox legal outside the US?

Usually, but not always, and LibriVox puts the check on you. Most countries count copyright from the author's death. In the UK, copyright in a book lasts 70 years after the author dies, and LibriVox's wiki notes that most countries in the Berne Convention use 50 or 70 years after death. So a book can be public domain in the US and still protected where you live.

A current example. *Winnie-the-Pooh* was published in 1926, so it entered the US public domain in 2022, and LibriVox now has six recordings of it. A. A. Milne died in 1956. Under the UK rule, his books stay in copyright there until 1 January 2027.

LibriVox's wiki explains how the date works with George Orwell, who died in 1950: his works entered the UK public domain on 1 January 2021, at the start of the 71st year after his death. Its Public Domain page also notes that the recordings are public domain in the US "but not necessarily in other countries."

On whose job this is, the wiki is explicit: "It is the responsibility of each reader and listener to determine whether a particular text is public domain in the country in which he or she resides." Each book's catalog page repeats it: if you are not in the USA, "please verify the copyright status of these works in your own country before downloading." If you are outside the US and the author died within the last 70 years, look up your country's rule before you download.

## Is LibriVox AI?

No. LibriVox's recording policy says: "LibriVox recordings are made by volunteers using their own voices." Since 2023, the ban on computer-generated content has also covered book summaries and cover images. Proof-listeners are told to flag any section that sounds synthesized, and when AI material is found, it "will be removed from the project or, if completed, the catalog."

There are two reasons people ask. LibriVox accepts every reader, "no matter what you sound like," so some readings can sound flat. And LibriVox audio has been turned into some large public datasets for speech AI:

- **LibriSpeech (2015)**, built for training and testing speech recognition, is 1,000 hours of read speech from LibriVox audiobooks.
- **Libri-Light (2020)** takes more than 60,000 hours of LibriVox audio. Its authors called it the largest freely available speech corpus they knew of.
- **LibriTTS (2019)**, built for text-to-speech, has 585 hours from 2,456 speakers, derived from LibriSpeech.

LibriVox answers this on its AI page. It "has not, does not, and will not sell its recordings to anyone," but because they are public domain, "it is legal for AI trainers to use them for anything without our/your permission, for free." The voices are human. AI has learned from them, not the other way round.

## Is LibriVox really free? What's the catch?

It is free: no subscription, no trial, no ads. Readers are unpaid volunteers, and the AI page describes LibriVox as "not a business with yearly goals, but a hobby for many people." It has run fund-raising drives (in 2010 and 2013), and the Internet Archive hosts its audio free of charge.

The catches:

- **Anyone may sell LibriVox recordings.** LibriVox says anyone can use them however they wish, "even to sell them." A paid app or store listing for an old classic may be charging for a recording you can get free.
- **Old books, unchanged.** Volunteers may not edit the published text, for example "to remove swear words, language or ideas you find objectionable." LibriVox warns that many of its books "may contain language or express notions that are antiquated at best, offending at worst." Worth knowing before you play one for a child.
- **Nothing recent.** Books published after 1930 are generally out of reach, so new releases and modern bestsellers are not there.

## Is LibriVox any good?

It varies, by design. LibriVox does not audition readers. Before a book enters the catalog, usually every file is proof-listened, but that check looks for mistakes such as "repeats, big stumbles or long pauses." Proof-listeners do not judge reading style, and their guide says they never tell anyone, "This reader is not good enough for LibriVox."

How to find a good recording:

1. **Prefer solo recordings.** The catalog labels each book Solo or Collaborative. A collaborative book passes between readers chapter by chapter, with different voices and microphones.
2. **Look for other versions.** Popular books are recorded more than once. *Winnie-the-Pooh* has six versions, including a dramatic reading.
3. **Play the first chapter** before you download the whole book.
4. **Adjust the speed.** When we timed ten LibriVox recordings, readers ranged from 131 to 179 words a minute, so a slow reader may sound better at 1.25x. The details are in [how long is an audiobook](/blog/how-long-is-an-audiobook).

## Why is LibriVox not working?

LibriVox runs on two separate systems, and either can fail on its own.

- **The catalog** at librivox.org, and the API that apps use to read it, run on a server that the volunteers also use to coordinate recordings. In September 2026, LibriVox announced limits on that API because traffic from "bots and scrapers," and from some apps, had become a problem for the server.
- **The audio** lives on the Internet Archive. When archive.org is down, LibriVox downloads stop even if the catalog loads. That happened in October 2024: after cyberattacks on October 9, the Internet Archive brought archive.org back in a read-only form on October 21.

Is LibriVox still available? Yes. It added 62 works to the catalog last month and marked its 20th anniversary in August 2025.

## LibriVox vs Libby and Audible

- **LibriVox** is for classics: public domain books, volunteer readers, free files you can keep, no account.
- **Libby** lends current ebooks and audiobooks from your public library. As its help pages put it: "All you need is a library card."
- **Audible** is a paid store and subscription for current audiobooks.

If LibriVox does not have the book you want, our guide to [listening to audiobooks for free](/blog/how-to-listen-to-audiobooks-for-free) compares nine legal options.

## What we could not confirm

- We are not lawyers. Copyright terms and their exceptions differ by country, so check an official source for yours.
- We found no independent study of LibriVox narration quality. The advice above comes from LibriVox's own rules.
- LibriVox's own guide says a project's files are "usually" all proof-listened before it enters the catalog. We could not find how often that check is skipped.

## Reading along with LibriVox in HushBook

**HushBook** is one of the third-party apps that use LibriVox's catalog. It has a built-in catalogue of public domain audiobooks from LibriVox and the Internet Archive, in six languages, and it shows who reads each book or flags one as read by several readers, so you can *pick a solo recording*. Download a book and **[HushBook](/)** transcribes it on your phone, then highlights each word as the volunteer reads it. Tap any word to jump the audio there. It is free to download, with **no ads and no account**. The read-along and the free library cost nothing, and an optional Pro upgrade adds advanced analytics. After a one-time engine download, it works offline.
