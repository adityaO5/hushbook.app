Are Audiobooks Good for Dyslexia? What the Research Says

Published October 20, 20267 min read

Key takeaways

  • Audiobooks let dyslexic readers take in books at the level of their thinking, not their decoding. They work best alongside structured reading instruction, not instead of it.
  • A 2018 meta-analysis of 22 studies found read-aloud tools improved reading comprehension for students with reading disabilities, with a small-to-moderate effect (d = 0.35).
  • Small trials link audiobooks and reading along with better motivation, well-being and fluency. In the largest trial, of 149 students, reading gains did not differ from usual teaching.
  • Yale and the British Dyslexia Association recommend following the words while listening, which keeps practice with print in the picture.
  • Dyslexia fonts such as OpenDyslexic showed no improvement in reading speed or accuracy in studies. Wider letter spacing looks more promising.

Why audiobooks help dyslexic readers

The International Dyslexia Association's definition, updated in October 2025, describes dyslexia as "difficulties in word reading and/or spelling that involve accuracy, speed, or both." It adds that "secondary consequences include reading comprehension problems and reduced reading and writing experience."

That second part is where audiobooks earn their place. A child or adult who reads slowly reads less, and reading less means meeting fewer words, ideas and stories. Listening breaks that loop. The IDA's own guidance says plainly that "students can benefit from listening to audiobooks."

It works because the hard part of dyslexia is usually turning print into words, not understanding language. In the classic model of reading by Gough and Tunmer, reading comprehension depends on two things: decoding and language comprehension. Dyslexia mainly affects the first. Some people with dyslexia also have wider language difficulties, so not everyone understands spoken text easily, but for many, listening comprehension is noticeably stronger than reading comprehension.

How common is it? The IDA says "perhaps as many as 15–20% of the population as a whole" have some symptoms of dyslexia. A 2022 meta-analysis put diagnosed dyslexia among primary school children at about 7%.

What the research says

  • Read-aloud tools improve comprehension, modestly. Wood and colleagues pooled 22 studies of students with reading disabilities, from grade 3 to adults, in a 2018 meta-analysis. Text-to-speech and read-aloud tools improved reading comprehension with an average effect size of d = 0.35. That is a small-to-moderate effect, and it shrank to 0.24 after correcting for publication bias.
  • Audiobooks may lift confidence and accuracy. In a 2010 Italian study by Milani, Lorusso and Molteni, 40 adolescents with dyslexia either used audiobooks or carried on with ordinary books for five months. The audiobook group improved in reading accuracy, showed less unease and fewer emotional and behavioural problems, and did better at school. It is a small study, so treat it as promising rather than settled.
  • Reading along can build fluency. In a 2011 study by Esteves and Whitten, 20 students with reading difficulties spent eight weeks of silent reading time following printed books while listening to the audiobook. Their reading speed rose from 84 to 101 words correct per minute, against 75 to 79 in the comparison group.
  • Audio does not replace reading instruction. In the largest trial here, 149 Swedish students with severe reading difficulties trained with text-to-speech and speech-to-text apps. Their reading gains did not differ from students who had their usual teaching, though motivation rose.
  • Audio is not always faster. A 2022 study of secondary students with dyslexia found that adding audio to a text made reading take longer and did not change comprehension. So audio is not automatically better for every reading task.

So the honest summary is: audiobooks give dyslexic readers access to books, help them keep up with what peers are reading, and seem to help motivation. They are not a treatment for dyslexia itself.

Does reading along with audiobooks help dyslexic readers?

It is what experts advise. The Yale Center for Dyslexia and Creativity tells teachers to "recommend that students read along with the audiobook version or to follow the words alongside the reader." The British Dyslexia Association notes that "some audio book apps will highlight the text on screen as it is being read, which can help your child identify words."

Reading along keeps the printed words in front of the reader, so they still see the spelling of each word they hear. Most audiobooks do not come with text, which is the gap apps like HushBook fill by making word-by-word text from the recording itself. That is the idea behind the fluency gains in the Esteves and Whitten study. Listening alone gives access to the story. Reading along adds practice with print.

Is listening to audiobooks cheating?

No. Listening is a different way into the same book, and for understanding a story the two come out about the same. We go through the evidence in does listening to an audiobook count as reading. For a dyslexic reader, the alternative to an audiobook is often not reading the book at all.

Audiobooks for kids with dyslexia

For children who are still learning to read, audiobooks belong next to reading lessons, not in place of them, and that holds for all young readers, not only those with dyslexia. Understood.org puts it simply: using assistive technology "doesn't keep people from learning to read," but these "tools alone will probably not improve reading skills."

A sensible split:

  • Reading lessons and practice at the child's decoding level, ideally with a structured, phonics-based approach.
  • Audiobooks for the books they want or need to read but cannot yet decode: class novels, series their friends are reading, non-fiction they are curious about.
  • Reading along with the text visible when possible, so listening time also becomes print time.

Audiobooks for adults with dyslexia

Adults have different goals: work documents, books for pleasure, study. Audiobooks make long books manageable. Reading along helps when you need to keep the text, quote it, or check a name you did not catch. And adjusting the speed lets you slow down for dense material and speed up for light reading.

Do dyslexia fonts help?

Not measurably. In a 2017 study by Wery and Diliberto, 12 students with dyslexia read in OpenDyslexic, Arial and Times New Roman, with "no improvement in reading rate or accuracy" from OpenDyslexic, and no student preferred it. A 2018 study by Kuster and colleagues tested the Dyslexie font with 170 children with dyslexia and found it did not make reading faster or more accurate either. Most preferred Arial.

Spacing is more promising. A 2012 study in PNAS by Zorzi and colleagues found extra-large letter spacing doubled reading accuracy for 74 children with dyslexia and made them slightly faster. Later studies found smaller effects that were not specific to dyslexia, so wider spacing may help some readers more than others.

The practical advice: use whatever font feels comfortable, and try wider letter and line spacing.

Frequently asked questions

Are audiobooks good for dyslexia?

Yes, as a companion to reading instruction. They let dyslexic readers take in books their decoding would block, and studies link them to better comprehension, motivation and well-being. They do not fix decoding on their own.

Does reading along with audiobooks help dyslexic readers?

It is what dyslexia experts at Yale and the British Dyslexia Association recommend. Seeing each word as it is spoken keeps print practice going, and one small study found reading fluency rose from 84 to 101 words correct per minute over eight weeks.

Is listening to audiobooks cheating if you have dyslexia?

No. Listening is a different way into the same book, and for understanding a story the two come out about the same. For many dyslexic readers the alternative is not reading the book at all.

Are audiobooks good for kids with dyslexia?

Yes, next to reading lessons, not in place of them. Use audiobooks for books a child wants or needs to read but cannot yet decode, and keep the text visible when possible.

Do dyslexia fonts like OpenDyslexic help?

Studies found no improvement in reading speed or accuracy from OpenDyslexic or Dyslexie. Use whichever font feels comfortable. Wider letter spacing helped accuracy in some studies.

Can audiobooks replace reading instruction for dyslexia?

No. In a trial of 149 students with severe reading difficulties, reading apps did not add reading gains over usual teaching. Audiobooks give access to books, and structured instruction builds reading skill.
