# Research: evidence for "Audiobooks With Captions"

Compiled 2026-11-03. **VERIFIED** = checked against the primary source (method noted). **UNVERIFIED** = not confirmed; do not cite as fact.

## A. Audible Captions history

**A1. Announcement (July 2019).** Audible newsroom, "Audible Captions: A Demonstration" (31 July 2019): "Audible Captions is a soon-to-launch technology that will provide, at user request, machine-generated transcriptions"; "Text is displayed progressively, and only a few lines at a time, while audio is playing"; "designed with students in mind"; launching "in September, at the start of the 2019 school year", free to "over 150,000 public school students"; "transcriptions are not an exact copy of the text, and errors may happen from time to time." PW (19 July 2019): Audible said it "does not replicate or replace the print or eBook reading experience." Authors Guild (18 July 2019): "appears to be outright, willful copyright infringement." **VERIFIED** (all three pages fetched).

**A2. Lawsuit (August 2019).** Filed 23 August 2019, U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York. Plaintiffs: Chronicle Books, Hachette Book Group, HarperCollins, Macmillan, Penguin Random House, Scholastic, Simon & Schuster (Publishing Perspectives, 30 August 2019). AAP CEO Maria Pallante: "We are extremely disappointed by Audible's deliberate disregard of authors, publishers, and copyright law" (PW). Captions had been due on 10 September. Per a court filing, Audible would not enable it for the plaintiffs' titles until Judge Valerie Caproni ruled on the preliminary injunction (hearing 25 September). **VERIFIED**. Docket 1:19-cv-07913 (search snippet only): **UNVERIFIED**.

**A3. Settlement and status.**
- Parties told the court on 13 January 2020 they "have resolved their disputes"; Judge Caproni dismissed the case on 14 January (PW, 14 January and 7 February 2020). **VERIFIED**.
- AAP statement (6 February 2020): "Audible has agreed that it will obtain permission from any AAP members that are in good standing", plus a consent permanent injunction. **VERIFIED**.
- PW (5 March 2020): the program "never launched"; "there are no plans to expand to the program beyond its current pilot", "limited to public domain works for a select group of high school students." Judge signed the final injunction 6 March. **VERIFIED**.
- **2026:** we found no Audible help page that mentions captions or subtitles (search, 3 November 2026; absence, not proof). "Accessibility at Audible" lists "Listen while you read: Read on your Kindle while you listen on Audible with real-time highlighting" and does not mention captions, transcripts or deaf users. **VERIFIED** (fetched).

**A4. Read & Listen.**
- Newsroom, 18 February 2026, "Audible Launches Immersion Reading for Deeper Engagement with Books": "real-time word-by-word text highlighting stays in sync with the audiobook narration", for customers who "have the audiobook and eBook versions of a title in their Audible and Kindle libraries." **VERIFIED**.
- Newsroom, 26 October 2026, "Audible's Read & Listen Experience Comes to More Listeners Around the Globe": "available for more than 800,000 English and German-language titles"; immersion reading, "already live in the U.S., UK, Germany, and Australia, is now rolling out to customers in Canada, India, France, Italy, Spain, Japan, and Brazil." **VERIFIED**.
- Help, "Learn about Read & Listen" (US and UK, 3 November 2026): "You will need both the audiobook and the Kindle eBook to access this feature." **VERIFIED**. Audible's read-along shows the purchased ebook text; it does not generate captions from the audio.

**A5. Speechify claim.** "Does Audible have subtitles?" (Cliff Weitzman, dated 6 May 2023): "tap on the gear icon in the top right corner. From there, select "Subtitles and Captions" and toggle the switch on or off". **VERIFIED** as Speechify's claim (Wayback snapshots 2023-07-16 and 2026-04-21). The live URL now redirects to speechify.com. Audible's pages do not support it: no such setting in any help or accessibility page, Captions never launched, and read-along needs the Kindle ebook. Safe line: "Some sites say Audible has a subtitles toggle. Audible's own help pages describe no such setting."

## B. Evidence

**B1. Gernsbacher (2015)**, "Video Captions Benefit Everyone", *Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 2(1):195–202. **VERIFIED** (Crossref; Europe PMC PMID 28066803; PMC full text). Narrative review, no pooled numbers: "More than 100 empirical studies document that captioning a video improves comprehension of, attention to, and memory for the video. Captions are particularly beneficial for persons watching videos in their non-native language, for children and adults learning to read, and for persons who are D/deaf or hard of hearing." Video only; nothing on noise or accents.

**B2. Caption meta-analyses**
- **Reynolds et al. (2022)**, *Systems* 10(5):133. **VERIFIED** (Crossref; full-text PDF). 139 studies screened, 34 reviewed, 20 meta-analysed. Overall random-effects d = 0.884 (95% CI 0.412–1.356; I² = 94.783, highly heterogeneous). Same-language captions (5 studies): d = 1.732, the largest effect. Language learners watching video.
- **Montero Perez et al. (2013)**, *System* 41(3):720–739. Title **VERIFIED** (Crossref). Search snippets of the abstract: 15 listening studies, 10 vocabulary studies, "a large effect" on both. Exact g values **UNVERIFIED** (paywalled); don't quote them.
- **Kothari, Pandey & Chudgar (2004)**, *Information Technologies and International Development* 2(1):23–44. **VERIFIED** (Crossref; abstract via OpenAlex). Same-language subtitles on a weekly Gujarati film-song show, 6 months: "an incremental but measurable contribution to decoding skills." Sample size **UNVERIFIED**.

**B3. Reading while listening**
- **Adesope & Nesbit (2012)**, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 104(1):250–263. **VERIFIED** (Crossref; abstract via OpenAlex). "57 independent studies", mostly postsecondary students. Spoken plus written text did no better than written-only, but beat spoken-only, especially "for low prior knowledge learners, system-paced learning materials, and picture-free materials". An audiobook is system-paced and picture-free. Pooled g **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Chang & Millett (2014)**, *ELT Journal* 68(1):31–40. **VERIFIED** (Crossref; abstract). Over 13 weeks, "the reading plus listening group produced the most consistent and significant outcome" against reading-only and listening-only. N **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Brown, Waring & Donkaewbua (2008)**, *Reading in a Foreign Language* 20(2):136–163. **VERIFIED** (Crossref abstract). "Most words were not learned"; after 3 months, "only 1 of the 28 items met in either of the reading modes and... none of the items met in the listening-only mode" were retained. N **UNVERIFIED**.

**B4. ADHD null result.** **Moussaoui et al. (2025)**, *Journal of the International Neuropsychological Society* 31(9–10):731–742. **VERIFIED** (Crossref; Europe PMC PMID 41277242; Cambridge full text). 76 undergraduates (38 ADHD, 38 controls). In PACED, "one word at a time" appeared "at its usual location", with the other words replaced by placeholder lines. RSVP showed one word at a time in the centre. ADHD readers did best with RSVP, gaining "almost 13% relative to neurotypical controls"; PACED did no better than normal text. No audio. **Correction:** PACED is not highlighting, since the other words are hidden. Write "one word at a time in its usual place, with the rest hidden."

**B5. Hearing loss**
- WHO *World report on hearing: executive summary* (2021): "Hearing loss currently affects more than 1.5 billion people worldwide, of whom 430 million have moderate or higher levels of hearing loss in the better hearing ear". **VERIFIED** (PDF).
- WHO release (2 March 2021): "Nearly 2.5 billion people worldwide ─ or 1 in 4 people ─ will be living with some degree of hearing loss by 2050"; "At least 700 million" will need rehabilitation. **VERIFIED**. The 2026 fact sheet gives 430 million but not 1.5 billion; cite the report for that.
- NIDCD, "Quick Statistics About Hearing, Balance, & Dizziness" (updated 20 September 2024): "Approximately 15% of American adults (37.5 million) ages 18 and over report some trouble hearing." Data: 2012 National Health Interview Survey. **VERIFIED**.

**B6. Caption use by hearing people (polls, not peer-reviewed)**
- **YouGov** (11 August 2023): 1,000 U.S. adults, 29 June–5 July 2023, opt-in panel. Prefer subtitles on: 63% under 30, 38% overall, 30% aged 65+. Reasons given by users: comprehension 40%, accents 40%, noisy environment 33%, hearing difficulties 30%. **VERIFIED**.
- **Preply** (tutoring vendor, 17 June 2022): 1,265 Americans. 50% use subtitles most of the time; Gen Z 70%; "Nearly 3 in 4" blame muddled audio. **VERIFIED**, but the page also gives 1,200 and "more than 1,260" as the sample.
- **Ofcom** (23 March 2006): "over 7.5 million people (18%) are estimated to have used subtitling at least once, of whom over 6 million people would have no hearing impairment." Ipsos MORI, 4,363 adults aged 15+. **VERIFIED** (PDF); 20-year-old data.

**B7. Noise and accents**
- **Zekveld et al. (2008)**, *Ear & Hearing* 29(6):838–852. Young normal-hearing listeners (n = 14, 10, 14 and 24 across experiments), sentences in noise. Machine-generated text that was only about 20% readable "improved the SRT by about 3 dB SNR", which equals "an increase in speech comprehension of about 35% in critical conditions". Delaying the text reduced the gain. **VERIFIED**.
- **Zekveld et al. (2009)**, *Ear & Hearing* 30(2):262–272. 22 young and 22 middle-aged adults with normal hearing, plus 30 middle-aged adults with hearing loss. "About 2 dB SNR audiovisual benefit for ASR accuracies around 74%"; benefit held "regardless of age and hearing loss". **VERIFIED**.
- **Mitterer & McQueen (2009)**, *PLoS ONE* 4(11):e7785. 121 Dutch students watched Scottish or Australian English. Afterwards they repeated new clips worse after Dutch subtitles and better after English subtitles, so same-language subtitles helped them learn the accent. **VERIFIED**.
- **Counterpoint: Zekveld et al. (2008)**, *Trends in Amplification* 13(1):44–68. Hearing-impaired users (n = 20) got delayed captions at 60–70% accuracy. They found "the erroneous text... difficult", and task load did not fall. Perceived benefit rose at 80–90% accuracy with no delay (n = 10). **VERIFIED**.

## C. What the evidence does not show
1. **The studies use video, not audiobooks**, mostly with language learners and students.
2. **Nobody has tested word-level audiobook captions with hard-of-hearing adults.** We found no such study. The closest is Zekveld's lab work with short sentences in noise.
3. **Accuracy and timing matter.** The benefit rises with transcript accuracy and falls with delay, and hearing-impaired users found error-filled text effortful. Don't imply that machine transcripts are perfect.
4. **Following along doesn't help everyone.** Spoken plus written text equalled written-only (Adesope & Nesbit). One word at a time in place did not help ADHD readers (Moussaoui). Reading while listening still left most new words unlearned (Brown).
5. **Usage numbers are self-reported** (opt-in panel, vendor survey, 2006 data). They show demand, not benefit.

## D. Accessibility bodies
- **WCAG 2.2 SC 1.2.1 Audio-only and Video-only (Prerecorded) (Level A)**, exact: "For prerecorded audio-only and prerecorded video-only media, the following are true, except when the audio or video is a media alternative for text and is clearly labeled as such: Prerecorded Audio-only: An alternative for time-based media is provided that presents equivalent information for prerecorded audio-only content. Prerecorded Video-only: Either an alternative for time-based media or an audio track is provided that presents equivalent information for prerecorded video-only content." **VERIFIED** (w3.org, version 12 December 2024). Understanding doc: "People who are deaf, are hard of hearing, or who are having trouble understanding audio information for any reason can read the text presentation." **VERIFIED**. **Caveat:** WCAG covers web content, and its "media alternative for text" exception can cover an audiobook of a print book. Say WCAG treats text as the accessible alternative to audio. Don't say it requires audiobook captions.
- **DAISY Knowledge Base**, "synchronizedAudioText": "a resource that offers both audio and text, with information that allows them to be rendered simultaneously". Named beneficiaries are language learners, blind and low-vision users, and readers who struggle to follow lines of text. It doesn't mention deaf readers. **VERIFIED**.
- **HLAA**: position paper "Free Access to High-Quality Captioning in Electronic Communications Platforms for People with Hearing Loss". It covers platforms, not audiobooks, and the page returned 404. **UNVERIFIED**.
- **NLS / RNIB**: talking-book services for blind and print-disabled readers. We found no statement from either on deaf readers. **UNVERIFIED**.
