# How to Read Classics Without Getting Bored: Why They Feel Hard and Where to Start

Make the book easier instead of trying harder. Boredom with a classic usually means the reading is too hard for the moment, or that you are reading it only because you feel you should. So start with a short classic in a genre you already like, learn a little about its world first, read in small daily doses, read the hardest passages aloud, and keep the text in front of you when you listen. If a book still drags after a few chapters, switch to another one.

The usual sticking points are long sentences, unfamiliar words, a world you don't know and a crowd of names in the first chapters. Your eyes reach the bottom of the page and nothing went in. ## Why are classics hard to read?

Because they were written for readers who lived in their world. The sentences are built differently, the vocabulary has moved on, and the author takes for granted things their first readers knew and you may not.

- **Longer sentences.** By our count of the Project Gutenberg texts, *A Tale of Two Cities* opens with a single sentence of 119 words. *Moby-Dick* averages about 24 words a sentence, against about 14 in *The Great Gatsby*, and one sentence in its chapter "The Whiteness of the Whale" runs to about 470 words. A long sentence asks you to keep several clauses in mind until it ends.
- **Old words and old meanings.** Words drift out of use or change meaning, and an older text uses them without a footnote.
- **Missing background.** The plot of *Pride and Prejudice* depends on Mr Bennet's estate being "entailed, in default of heirs male, on a distant relation." Austen's first readers knew what that meant. In a 1988 study, Recht and Leslie had 64 junior high students read an account of half an inning of a baseball game. Students who knew a lot about baseball remembered more on every measure, whether they were strong or weak readers.
- **The setup comes first.** The opening chapters introduce the world and most of the cast, and the start is where losing focus costs most. In a 2008 study of people reading a detective novella, Smallwood, McSpadden and Schooler found that zoning out had the greatest effect when it happened early in the story.

## How to read classics without getting bored

Treat boredom as information about the reading, not about you. In Westgate and Wilson's MAC model, published in *Psychological Review* in 2018, boredom comes from two kinds of mismatch: between what a task demands and the attention you have for it, whether it asks too little or too much, and between the activity and the goals you value. A classic can miss on both counts: too dense to hold your attention, and read only because you feel you ought to.

Difficulty also feeds mind wandering. In a 2013 study by Feng, D'Mello and Graesser, people read easy or difficult versions of eight passages. Their minds wandered more often on the difficult versions, and there the wandering did more harm to comprehension.

So fix the mismatch:

1. **Pick a classic you want to read.** Start from a genre you already like: Sherlock Holmes for crime, *Frankenstein* or *Dracula* for horror, *The Call of the Wild* or *Treasure Island* for adventure, *Pride and Prejudice* for romance. That covers the meaning half of the model.
2. **Start short.** A book you can finish in a week or two keeps the end in sight. The list below starts at under three hours of listening.
3. **Learn the world first.** Read the edition's introduction, or a page about the author and the period. Introductions sometimes discuss the ending, so stop when they turn to the plot. That background is exactly what the baseball study measured.
4. **Give the opening chapters your full attention.** Read them somewhere quiet and go back over any page you drifted through. In the novella study, early lapses cost the most.
5. **Read hard passages aloud.** In a 2013 study of 211 students, Varao Sousa, Carriere and Smilek compared reading aloud, reading silently and listening. Reading aloud led to the least mind wandering. It suits plays, poetry and dense dialogue.
6. **Read and listen at the same time.** A narrator carries the rhythm of a long sentence, and the text shows you what you missed. More on this below.
7. **Keep a cast list.** Write each new name with one line on who they are. Our guide to [keeping track of characters](/blog/how-to-keep-track-of-characters-in-a-book) covers the method, including *War and Peace*.
8. **Change the translation, not the book.** Free texts and recordings of translated classics usually use old translations, because every translation has its own copyright. LibriVox's example is Kafka's *The Metamorphosis*: the German original is public domain in the US, while David Wyllie's 2002 English translation is not. If an old translation feels stiff, borrow a modern one.
9. **Read a little every day.** At 20 minutes a day, the 13 hours 25 minutes of LibriVox's solo *Pride and Prejudice* take about six weeks.

## What are the easiest classics to read for beginners?

Short, plot-driven books with plain sentences. These six are free LibriVox recordings, each read by one volunteer, and LibriVox only records books that are public domain in the US. Lengths are the LibriVox running times; words a sentence are our count of the Project Gutenberg text.

- ***A Christmas Carol*** by Charles Dickens: 2 hours 44 minutes, read by Glen Hallstrom, about 16 words a sentence. Five short staves, and a ghost story you probably know the shape of.
- ***The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes*** by Arthur Conan Doyle: twelve stories in 13 hours 29 minutes, read by Ruth Golding, so a little over an hour a story; about 16 words a sentence. You finish a whole mystery in one sitting.
- ***The Call of the Wild*** by Jack London: 3 hours 34 minutes, read by Tom Crawford, about 19 words a sentence. A dog's adventure set in the Klondike gold rush.
- ***The Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde*** by Robert Louis Stevenson: 3 hours 12 minutes, read by Bob Neufeld, about 23 words a sentence. Denser prose, but short, with a famous twist.
- ***The Great Gatsby*** by F. Scott Fitzgerald: 5 hours 38 minutes, read by Kara Shallenberg, about 14 words a sentence, the shortest of the six.
- ***Frankenstein*** by Mary Shelley: 7 hours 26 minutes, read by Caden Vaughn Clegg, about 23 words a sentence. A good second step once a short one has gone well.

For comparison, *Pride and Prejudice* averages about 21 words a sentence and runs 13 hours 25 minutes on LibriVox, and *Moby-Dick* about 24 words and 24 hours 38 minutes. Our guide to [how long an audiobook is](/blog/how-long-is-an-audiobook) lists more classics with their listening times.

## Are classics better as audiobooks?

Often, as long as you keep the text close. A good narrator carries a long sentence to its end and gives each character a voice. Listening alone, though, is where minds wander most. In the 211-student study above, listening to a passage produced the most mind wandering, the poorest memory for it and the least interest in it.

Seeing and hearing the words together does better. Adesope and Nesbit's 2012 meta-analysis of 57 studies, mostly with college students, found that people who got spoken and written words together outperformed people who only heard them. The advantage showed up for learners with little prior knowledge and for material that moved at a fixed pace. A classic on audio is often both: an unfamiliar world, read at the narrator's speed. Our guide to [whether reading along with an audiobook helps](/blog/does-reading-along-with-an-audiobook-help) covers the rest of that research.

Three checks before you press play:

- **Pick the narrator.** LibriVox readers are volunteers and quality varies, so prefer a solo recording and play the first chapter. More in [is LibriVox safe and legal](/blog/is-librivox-safe-and-legal).
- **Make sure it's unabridged.** An abridged recording cuts description and side plots, so it won't match a full text. See [abridged vs unabridged audiobooks](/blog/abridged-vs-unabridged-audiobooks).
- **Slow down for dense prose.** Our [listening speed guide](/blog/audiobook-listening-speed) suggests 1x to 1.25x for literary fiction.

## How to get into classic literature

Build up over a few books instead of starting with the biggest one on the list.

1. **First book:** one of the six above, in a genre you like.
2. **Second book:** a longer one in the same vein. From the Holmes stories to *The Hound of the Baskervilles*, or from *Jekyll and Hyde* to *Frankenstein* or *Dracula*.
3. **Third book:** a big novel with support, such as *Pride and Prejudice* or *Jane Eyre*, with the audio, the text and a cast list.
4. **Then the giants.** *War and Peace* runs 62 hours 27 minutes across the four volumes of LibriVox's recording of the Maude translation, so plan it like a long series: a few chapters a night.

## What the research does not show

- None of these studies tested people reading a whole classic novel. They used short passages, a baseball account, a detective novella and lab tasks.
- The baseball study tested junior high students and sports knowledge. Applying it to the background of a period novel is our step.
- The MAC model explains boredom in general. It has not been tested on reading classics.
- Our sentence counts use a simple rule: a sentence ends at a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark followed by a capital letter. Books full of short dialogue come out lower. Treat the figures as rough comparisons.
