## 1. Why can't I focus on reading anymore? (the short answer, with the usual causes)

**Findings**

- **Drifting off is normal, not a personal failing.** In a 2013 study of 211 University of Waterloo students (two samples of 106 and 105 analysed, 235 recruited), Varao Sousa, Carriere and Smilek probed readers at random moments. Minds were off the text at about a third of probes during silent reading (M = 0.356) and about half during listening (M = 0.506). Reading aloud produced the least mind wandering. **VERIFIED** (PMC3842750 full text). Quote: "Here we see similar rates of mind wandering for Listening (M = 0.506) and Reading Silently (M = 0.356) as reported by other studies."
- **The longer you read, the more you drift.** Zanesco, Denkova and Jha's 2025 individual-participant meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin* pooled 68 research reports, "almost a half-million total responses" to thought probes from "more than 10,000 unique individuals", and found "that mind-wandering increases in frequency over time during task performance". It also cites the general estimate that mind wandering fills "between 30% and 50% of our waking moments". **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Caveat: a range of lab tasks, not reading only.
- **Hard text pulls attention away more often.** In a 2013 study of 80 undergraduates, Feng, D'Mello and Graesser found mind wandering at 42.3% of probes on difficult texts versus 36.2% on easy versions of the same passages; readers were "1.24 (e^0.212) times more likely to mind wander when reading a difficult than when reading an easy sentence". Mind wandering also hurt comprehension more on the hard texts (61.9% correct on easy texts, 56.2% on difficult). **VERIFIED** (Springer open-access PDF).
- **Interest and motivation matter.** Unsworth and McMillan (2013) found that "variation in mind wandering while reading was influenced by working memory capacity, topic interest, and motivation". **VERIFIED (abstract)**; sample size **UNVERIFIED** (APA PsycNet paywall). In Varao Sousa 2013, mind wandering and interest correlated at r = -0.43 and -0.42 for silent reading in the two samples. **VERIFIED** (PMC table).
- **Drifting costs comprehension, modestly.** A 2023 meta-analysis by Bonifacci and colleagues (25 studies, 73 correlations) found a pooled correlation of r = -0.21 between mind wandering and reading comprehension. When mind wandering was measured by a trait questionnaire instead of in-the-moment probes, the link disappeared. **VERIFIED (abstract)**; PMC9971160 full text available.
- **Tiredness hits attention hardest.** Lim and Dinges' 2010 meta-analysis of 70 articles (147 cognitive tests) on short-term sleep deprivation found effects "from small and nonsignificant (reasoning accuracy: g = -0.125 ...) to large (lapses in simple attention: g = -0.776 ...)". **VERIFIED (abstract)**; PMC3290659 open.
- **Short sleep adds up without feeling like it.** In Van Dongen and colleagues' 2003 lab study of 48 healthy adults aged 21 to 38, sleeping 6 hours or less a night for 14 days "produced cognitive performance deficits equivalent to up to 2 nights of total sleep deprivation", and "subjects were largely unaware of these increasing cognitive deficits". **VERIFIED (abstract)**.
- **Stress shrinks working memory a little.** Shields, Sazma and Yonelinas' 2016 meta-analysis found acute stress impaired working memory (34 studies, 164 effects, N = 1,353; g = -0.197, 95% CI -0.330 to -0.064). **VERIFIED** (PMC5003767 full text). Lab-induced acute stress; small effect.
- **Low mood makes the mind wander.** Smallwood, Fitzgerald, Miles and Phillips (2009) induced moods before a sustained attention task: a negative mood led participants "to make more lapses, report a greater frequency of task irrelevant thoughts, and become less inclined to reengage attentional resources following a lapse". **VERIFIED (abstract)**; n not in the abstract.

**Use in post:** "Some drifting is built into reading. In a 2013 study of 211 students, Varao Sousa, Carriere and Smilek found that minds were off the page at about a third of random check-ins during silent reading. Drifting goes up when the text is harder, the topic is less interesting, you are short of sleep, or you have been reading for a while."

## 2. Why can't I focus when I read but I can watch TV or scroll? (passive versus self-paced attention)

**Findings**

- **Reading only moves when you move it.** Smallwood's 2011 review: "When the mind wanders to unrelated thoughts and feelings while reading, the eyes often continue to scan the words without due attention to their meaning." Reading needs the reader to keep attention coupled to the text; nothing outside pulls it forward. **VERIFIED (abstract)**.
- **Being paced is not the same as paying attention.** Listening to a passage (paced by the speaker) produced more mind wandering than silent reading (0.506 vs 0.356) in Varao Sousa 2013. So TV holding you is not simply "external pacing makes focus easy". **VERIFIED**.
- **No direct comparison exists.** We found no study comparing mind wandering while watching TV with mind wandering while reading. Say so; do not present "TV is passive attention" as an established finding.
- **Screens train fast switching at work.** In a 2016 CHI study, Mark, Iqbal, Czerwinski, Johns and Sano logged the computers of 40 information workers at a US tech company for about 12 business days. Average focus on one screen before switching was 47.0 seconds (median 40.2). Shorter focus was associated with lower self-rated productivity at day's end. **VERIFIED** (ACM open PDF, Table 1). Quote: "the median duration of focus is about 40 seconds."
- **Heavy short-video use goes with weaker attention; direction unknown.** Nguyen and colleagues' 2025 meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin* (71 studies, 98,299 participants) found heavier short-form video use associated with poorer attention (r = -.38) and inhibitory control (r = -.41). **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Correlational: it cannot say whether scrolling weakens attention or people with weaker attention scroll more.

**Use in post:** "A show or a feed moves on its own and changes every few seconds; a book only moves when you do. When your mind slips during reading, your eyes can keep going without taking anything in, which is how a page passes unread. Being paced is not a cure on its own, though: in the same 2013 study, listening produced more mind wandering than silent reading."

**Sources**
- [Smallwood (2011), "Mind-wandering While Reading: Attentional Decoupling, Mindless Reading and the Cascade Model of Inattention", Language and Linguistics Compass](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-818X.2010.00263.x)
- [Mark, Iqbal, Czerwinski, Johns and Sano (2016), "Neurotics Can't Focus: An in situ Study of Online Multitasking in the Workplace", Proceedings of the 2016 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858202)
- [Nguyen et al. (2025), "Feeds, feelings, and focus: A systematic review and meta-analysis examining the cognitive and mental health correlates of short-form video use", Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000498)

## 3. Is my phone ruining my attention span? (screen-switching research, the goldfish myth, the reading decline)

**Findings: phone nearby ("brain drain")**

- **Original finding.** Ward, Duke, Gneezy and Bos (2017) ran two experiments with "nearly 800 smartphone users" (UT Austin release on ScienceDaily, June 23, 2017). People whose phones were in another room outperformed those whose phones sat face down on the desk on working memory and fluid intelligence tasks. The abstract says the effect appeared "even when people are successful at maintaining sustained attention". **VERIFIED (abstract + press release)**; per-experiment n **UNVERIFIED** (publisher and repository blocked).
- **Direct replications failed.** Ruiz Pardo and Minda (2022), a pre-registered direct replication of Ward's Experiment 2 (six conditions: desk, pocket or bag, outside the room; phone on or off): "the 'brain drain' effect did not replicate". **VERIFIED (abstract)**; n **UNVERIFIED**. Hartmann, Martarelli, Reber and Rothen (2020): "We found no overall effect of smartphone presence on short-term and prospective memory performance." **VERIFIED (abstract)**; n **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Meta-analyses: small to none.** Parry (2024, *Media Psychology*; k = 56, n = 7,093): only working memory capacity showed a significant pooled effect, "smaller than theorized", with "generally poor statistical power". **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Hartanto and colleagues (2024, *Technology, Mind, and Behavior*; 33 studies, 53 samples, 4,368 participants, 166 effect sizes): "the mere presence of smartphone had no significant effect on cognitive outcomes (d = -0.02, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [-0.06, 0.01], p = .246)". **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Böttger, Poschik and Zierer (2023; 22 studies, 43 effects) found an overall negative effect, but pooled smartphone *use* with presence. **VERIFIED (abstract)**.
- **Notifications are a clearer cost.** Stothart, Mitchum and Yehnert (2015): "cellular phone notifications alone significantly disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task, even when participants did not directly interact with a mobile device during the task", with distraction "comparable in magnitude" to calling or texting. **VERIFIED (abstract)**; n not in the abstract.
- **Switching costs time.** Rubinstein, Meyer and Evans (2001): alternating tasks "yielded switching-time costs that increased with rule complexity". **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Lab tasks; background only.

**Findings: the "47 seconds" figure (what Gloria Mark measured)**

- APA *Speaking of Psychology*, Episode 225 (February 2023), "Why our attention spans are shrinking, with Gloria Mark, PhD". Mark: "So back in 2004, we found the average attention span on any screen to be two and a half minutes on average. Throughout the years it became shorter. So around 2012 we found it to be 75 seconds. This is with logging techniques. This is an average. And then in the last five, six years, we found it to average about 47 seconds, and others have replicated this result within a few seconds." Also: "The median is 40 seconds." **VERIFIED** (Wayback snapshot 2026-10-10; live page bot-blocked). The interviewer frames it as "at least as measured by how long people spend on tasks and screens at work".
- **What was measured:** how long office workers stay on one computer window before switching, first by stopwatch shadowing (2004), then by logging software. Mark 2016 (40 workers) is the closest primary: mean 47.0 s, median 40.2 s. **VERIFIED**. The 2004 stopwatch study is Mark, Gonzalez and Harris (2005, CHI), 24 information workers; its abstract says "57% of their working spheres are interrupted" but does not give the 2.5-minute figure. **The 2.5-minute and 75-second figures: VERIFIED as Mark's statements, UNVERIFIED in a primary paper.**
- Her book: *Attention Span: A Groundbreaking Way to Restore Balance, Happiness and Productivity* (Hanover Square Press, January 2023; ISBN 9781335449412 via Open Library). Not read.
- **Not a measure of reading attention or of attention capacity.** It is switching behaviour at a work computer.

**Findings: the goldfish myth**

- The claim: Time, May 14, 2015, "You Now Have a Shorter Attention Span Than a Goldfish": "according to a new study from Microsoft Corp., people now generally lose concentration after eight seconds". **VERIFIED** (fetched).
- The debunk: BBC News, "Busting the attention span myth" by Simon Maybin (BBC World Service, More or Less), published 10 March 2017. The Microsoft Canada report (2,000 Canadians surveyed, brain activity of 112 people) did not produce the figure: "It appears in the report, but with a citation for another source called Statistic Brain." The sources Statistic Brain listed could not "find any record of research that backs up the stats". Dr Gemma Briggs (Open University): "It's very much task-dependent." Conclusion: "So goldfish don't have short attention spans or memories. There is no evidence human attentions spans are shrinking." **VERIFIED** (fetched; "attentions" is the BBC's typo).
- The lecture version of the myth: Bradbury (2016) found "the available primary data do not support the concept of a 10- to 15-min attention limit", and the greatest variability in student attention came from "differences between teachers". **VERIFIED (abstract)**.
- What is measured: collective attention to topics is getting shorter. Lorenz-Spreen, Mønsted, Hövel and Lehmann (2019, *Nature Communications*) found "shortened periods in the trajectories of how cultural items receive collective attention" across decades of data. **VERIFIED (abstract)**. That is how fast public topics rise and fall, not how long one person can concentrate.

**Findings: reading for pleasure is declining**

- **US daily reading, 2003 to 2023.** Bone, Bu, Sonke and Fancourt (2025, *iScience*) analysed the American Time Use Survey (n = 236,270). The share reading for pleasure on a given day fell "from highs of 28% in 2004 to lows of 16% in 2023; a relative decrease of 3% per year" (prevalence ratio 0.97). People who did read spent more time, "from 1 h to 23 min a day in 2003 to 1 h and 37 min in 2023". ATUS counts "listening to audiobooks" as reading for personal interest. Whether digital distraction drives the fall was "beyond the scope of our study". **VERIFIED** (PMC12496190 full text).
- **US adults, any book.** NEA, "Arts Participation Patterns in 2022: Highlights from the Survey of Public Participation in the Arts" (October 2023; 40,718 adults): read any book in the past 12 months 54.6% (2012), 52.7% (2017), 48.5% (2022); novels or short stories 45.2%, 41.8%, 37.6%, "the lowest share on record with the survey". **VERIFIED** (PDF).
- **Pew (balance).** Pew Research Center, "Three-in-ten Americans now read e-books" (January 6, 2022): "75% of U.S. adults say they have read a book in the past 12 months in any format, whether completely or part way through, a figure that has remained largely unchanged since 2011" (1,502 adults, January 25 to February 8, 2021). **VERIFIED**. Different question (any book in a year) from ATUS (reading on a given day).
- **US teens.** NAEP Long-Term Trend 2023: "In 2023, fourteen percent of students reported reading for fun almost every day" (13-year-olds), "13 percentage points lower than 2012". **VERIFIED** (nationsreportcard.gov highlights page). Monitoring the Future, as cited by Bone 2025: daily book or magazine reading among 17 to 18 year-olds fell "from 60% in the late 1970s to 16% by 2016" (Twenge, Martin and Spitzberg 2019). **SECONDARY** (primary abstract not available).
- **UK children.** National Literacy Trust, "Children and young people's reading in 2025" (114,970 responses, early 2025): "Just 1 in 3 (32.7%) children and young people aged 8 to 18 said that they enjoyed reading in their free time in 2025. This marks a 36% decrease in reading enjoyment levels since we started asking about this in 2005"; daily reading 18.7%. **VERIFIED**. **Newer:** "Children and young people's reading in 2026" (added 9 June 2026; 125,375 respondents, 479 schools): enjoyment rose to 36.1%, "the first increase in reading enjoyment since 2021"; daily reading 20.3% (ages 8 to 18). **VERIFIED**. Use the 2026 figures with the 2025 low as context. (A DJS Research summary says 20.5% daily; NLT's own page says 20.3%.)

**Use in post:** "The claim that people now have an eight-second attention span, shorter than a goldfish, has no traceable source; a 2017 BBC investigation found the figure in a Microsoft Canada report was credited to a website whose listed sources had no record of it. What has been measured is narrower. Gloria Mark's logging studies found office workers switch computer windows about every 47 seconds on average, and the American Time Use Survey shows the share of Americans reading for pleasure on a given day fell from 28% in 2004 to 16% in 2023. Whether a phone nearby drains your focus is contested: the 2017 'brain drain' study did not replicate, and a 2024 meta-analysis of 33 studies found no effect, while notifications reliably distract."

## 4. Can't focus on reading: ADHD, anxiety, depression or your eyes? (when to see someone)

**Findings**

- **ADHD.** NIMH, "Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder: What You Need to Know" (NIH Publication No. 24-MH-8300, revised 2024). Signs of inattention include frequent difficulty "Concentrating on tasks or activities, for instance, while having conversations, taking tests, completing assignments, or reading papers" and "Getting easily distracted by unrelated thoughts or stimuli". Key for "anymore": "ADHD symptoms must begin in childhood (before age 12)." Adults and youth over 16 need at least five symptoms, present for at least 6 months, in two or more settings. **VERIFIED**. So focus trouble that began recently in adulthood is not, by itself, a sign of ADHD; an assessment looks back to childhood.
- **ADHD and mind wandering** (reuse from the ADHD post): Seli, Smallwood, Cheyne and Smilek (2015), 2,708 university students, correlations of 0.52 and 0.47 between ADHD symptoms and spontaneous mind wandering. Title **VERIFIED** (Crossref); numbers as already stated in `are-audiobooks-good-for-adhd.md`, not re-read this session.
- **Depression.** NIMH, "Depression" (NIH Publication No. 24-MH-8079, revised 2024) lists "Difficulty concentrating, remembering, or making decisions"; diagnosis needs symptoms "most of the day, nearly every day, for at least 2 weeks". **VERIFIED**.
- **Anxiety.** NIMH, "Generalized Anxiety Disorder: What You Need to Know" (revised 2025): people with GAD may "Have a hard time concentrating"; diagnosis needs worry that is difficult to control "on most days for at least 6 months" plus at least three symptoms, which include "trouble concentrating". **VERIFIED**.
- **Eyes after 40 (presbyopia).** American Academy of Ophthalmology, "What Is Presbyopia?" (by David Turbert, reviewed by Arjan S Hura, MD; page dated May 21, 2026): "You may start to notice presbyopia shortly after age 40. You will probably find that you hold reading materials farther away in order to see them clearly." **VERIFIED**.
- **Eye teaming (convergence insufficiency).** National Eye Institute, "Convergence Insufficiency" (last updated December 4, 2024): the most common symptoms include "Trouble concentrating"; "You might lose your place, read slowly, or feel like the words move or float around on the page." **VERIFIED**. AAO's page: "most common in children and young adults". **VERIFIED**.
- **Long COVID.** NHS, "Long COVID" (last reviewed 19 August 2025): the most common symptoms include "problems with your memory and concentration, also called brain fog". **VERIFIED**.

**Use in post:** "Some reading trouble needs a professional, not a reading trick. NIMH lists difficulty concentrating as a symptom of depression (lasting 2 weeks or more) and of generalised anxiety (6 months or more). ADHD symptoms, by definition, start before age 12. If print blurs up close after about 40, the American Academy of Ophthalmology says that is presbyopia, and if words seem to float or you keep losing your place, the National Eye Institute lists convergence insufficiency. This guide is a reading aid, not medical advice."

## 5. Why do I read the same page over and over? (mindless reading)

**Findings**

- **The eyes keep moving after the mind leaves.** Reichle, Reineberg and Schooler (2010, *Psychological Science*): fixations during mindless reading "were longer and less affected by lexical and linguistic variables than fixations during the former", and "eye movements immediately preceding self-caught mind wandering were especially erratic". **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Sample, from the APS release (August 30, 2010): "Four undergraduate students at the University of Pittsburgh volunteered for the project. Each one came to the lab for a dozen or more one-hour reading sessions of Jane Austen's *Sense and Sensibility*, chosen because it's 'fairly easy but a little bit dry'"; Reichle: "We started with Kafka's *The Trial*, but people found it too engaging." **VERIFIED** (APS release). Describe it as "a small eye-tracking study with many hours of reading per person".
- **You often notice late.** Smallwood 2011 (quote in section 2). Smallwood, Fishman and Schooler (2007) argue that mind wandering impairs "the building of a narrative model with sufficient detail to allow generating inferences", and that "recognizing and correcting for mind wandering is a metacognitive skill". **VERIFIED (abstract)**. A theoretical review with no sample.
- **Going back is normal.** Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter and Treiman (2016, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*): "About 10% to 15% of the time, skilled readers make regressions, moving backward in the text to a previous word", and "many regressions are made to correct a failure in comprehension". Also: "Readers are often able to fix comprehension problems by rereading, something that speed-reading training courses discourage". **VERIFIED** (open SAGE PDF).
- **Harder pages lose you more often** (Feng 2013, section 1).

**Use in post:** "Reaching the bottom of a page with no idea what it said is common enough to have a name: mindless reading. In a small 2010 eye-tracking study, Reichle, Reineberg and Schooler found that readers' eyes kept moving along the lines while their minds were elsewhere, just in a slower way that no longer responded to the words. Going back is not a failure: skilled readers move their eyes backwards 10% to 15% of the time, often to repair a sentence they missed."

## 7. How to get out of a reading slump

**Findings**

- **No research on "reading slumps" as such.** Crossref and Europe PMC searches found no study of adult reading slumps and no trial of any slump remedy. State this plainly.
- **Wanting to read predicts reading more.** Schiefele, Schaffner, Möller and Wigfield's 2012 review in *Reading Research Quarterly*: evidence "confirms the positive contribution of intrinsic reading motivation ... to reading behavior and reading competence", "particularly evident in relation to amount of reading for enjoyment", but "the causal role of reading motivation and the mediating role of reading behavior remain largely unresolved issues". **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Mostly school-age research.
- **Interest lowers drift** (Unsworth and McMillan 2013; Varao Sousa 2013; sections 1 and 2).
- **Routine helps, slowly.** Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts and Wardle (2010, *European Journal of Social Psychology*): 96 volunteers repeated a chosen daily behaviour "in the same context (for example 'after breakfast')" for 12 weeks; 82 gave enough data and the model fitted 62 (39 well). Time to reach 95% of peak automaticity "ranged from 18 to 254 days"; "Missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process." **VERIFIED (abstract)**. The famous 66 days: **SECONDARY** (BPS Research Digest: "The average time to reach maximum automaticity was 66 days"); not in the abstract. Behaviours were eating, drinking or activity, not reading.
- Daniel Pennac's *The Rights of the Reader* (English edition 2006, Walker Books and Candlewick; Open Library) is often quoted for "the right not to finish a book". Edition **VERIFIED**; exact wording **UNVERIFIED** (not read). Optional colour only.

**Use in post:** "There is no study of reading slumps as such, so be wary of any five-step cure. What research does support is wanting to read: Schiefele and colleagues' 2012 review found that reading for its own sake goes with reading more. For the routine, a 2010 habit study by Lally and colleagues found a daily behaviour tied to a cue became automatic anywhere from 18 to 254 days in, and missing a single day made no real difference."

## 8. How to focus on reading again: 9 things that help

Evidence strength: **Good** = meta-analysis or several consistent studies; **Some** = one or two studies, or indirect; **Thin** = theory or anecdote; **Mixed** = studies disagree.

1. **Short timed sessions.** *Some (indirect).* Mind wandering rises with time on task (Zanesco 2025, 68 reports, more than 10,000 people). No trial of reading session length found. Gloria Mark on the Pomodoro timer (APA 2023): "Well, that's fine, but there are individual differences and some people would be able to work longer than what the Pomodoro technique dictates. Some people would work less." **VERIFIED**. Don't cite spaced-practice research (Cepeda 2006) here: it is about memory, not focus.
2. **A shorter or easier book.** *Some.* Mind wandering was more frequent on difficult texts (Feng 2013, 80 students, 42.3% vs 36.2%), and interest predicts less drifting (Unsworth and McMillan 2013). No study on book length.
3. **Phone in another room, notifications off.** *Mixed for presence; some for notifications.* Ward 2017 found a benefit; the direct replication and the 2024 meta-analyses found none, or a small working memory effect. Notifications alone disrupted attention (Stothart 2015). Cheap to try; say the evidence is contested.
4. **Read a page aloud.** *Mixed for focus; good for memory.* Varao Sousa 2013: least mind wandering when reading aloud. Franklin, Mooneyham, Baird and Schooler (2014): "participants reported more mind-wandering while they were reading aloud", and those lapses came with "nearly normal prosody". **VERIFIED (abstract)**. For memory, the production effect is solid: MacLeod and colleagues (2010), 8 experiments, found producing a word aloud "improves explicit memory", though the effect was "restricted to within-subject, mixed-list designs" where only some items are said aloud. **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Ozubko, Hourihan and MacLeod (2012), with essays: "Memory was superior for questions that probed information that had been read aloud relative to information that had been read silently", and the benefit lasted a week. **VERIFIED (abstract)**. So: aloud for a key passage, not the whole book.
5. **Paper for long or hard text.** *Good, small, task-dependent.* Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman and Salmerón (2018): 54 studies (38 between-participants, 16 within) with 171,055 participants; paper beat screens, g = -0.21. The paper advantage "increased in time-constrained reading compared to self-paced reading", held for informational texts "but not on those using only narrative texts", and grew with publication year. **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Clinton (2019): 33 studies, n = 2,799; screens g = -.25; expository g = -.32; narrative g = -.04 (no difference); no reliable difference in reading time (g = .08); judgement of one's own performance better calibrated on paper (g = .20). **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Singer and Alexander (2017) review: medium matters "under certain text or task conditions or for certain readers". **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Limits: mostly students, short expository texts, comprehension tests; not focus or enjoyment of novels.
6. **Read along with the audiobook.** *Some, with limits.* See section 9.
7. **A pacer or finger under the line.** *Thin.* We found no controlled study of finger or pacer reading for adult focus. Rayner and colleagues (2016) on speed reading: "It is unlikely that readers will be able to double or triple their reading speeds (e.g., from around 250 to 500-750 words per minute) while still being able to understand the text as well as if they read at normal speed." **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Present as "some people like it; no evidence either way", and don't promise speed.
8. **A question per chapter.** *Some, mostly from lectures.* Pan, Schmitt, Bjork and Sana (2020): with a 26-minute online lecture, "Taking pretests reduced mind wandering and improved performance on a subsequent final test compared to the control condition." **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Welhaf, Phillips, Smeekens, Miyake and Kane (2022), 195 US undergraduates: interpolated testing reduced off-task thoughts "but with a small effect size and no supporting Bayes-factor evidence", and there was no effect of matched pretesting. **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Pan and Carpenter's 2023 review: pre-questions "can often enhance learning, but the extent of that enhancement may vary". **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated practice testing and distributed practice "high utility", elaborative interrogation and self-explanation "moderate utility", and rereading and highlighting "low utility". **VERIFIED (abstract)**. All study-focused; translate as "ask yourself one question before a chapter and answer it after".
9. **Same time each day.** *Some (indirect).* Lally 2010, section 7. Not tested for reading.

**Use in post (lead-in to the list):** "None of these is a cure, and several have only indirect evidence. The best-supported moves are the simplest: read in shorter sittings, because mind wandering climbs the longer any task runs; pick a book that is easier or more interesting for now, because hard text pulls attention away more often; and silence notifications, which disrupted attention in a 2015 lab study even when people never touched the phone."

## 9. Does reading along with an audiobook help you focus?

**Findings** (keep short; link `/blog/does-reading-along-with-an-audiobook-help`)

- **Listening alone drifts most.** Varao Sousa 2013: listening 0.506 vs silent reading 0.356; listening also gave the worst memory and the least interest. **VERIFIED**.
- **Reading along vs reading alone: small and pace-dependent.** Clinton-Lisell (2023), 30 studies, total N = 1,945: "a trivial overall benefit of reading while listening over reading only on comprehension was found g = 0.18"; experimenter-paced g = 0.41; self-paced g = 0.06 (not reliable). **VERIFIED** (ERIC record EJ1403866 fetched).
- **Reading along vs listening alone: better.** Adesope and Nesbit (2012), 57 studies: spoken plus written beat spoken only, especially for "low prior knowledge learners, system-paced learning materials, and picture-free materials". Title **VERIFIED** (Crossref); wording reused from the read-along post's research.
- **Narrator pace is slower than reading pace.** Our ten-classic LibriVox count: median 151 wpm (range 131 to 179), from `how-long-is-an-audiobook.md`. Brysbaert (2019): silent reading 238 wpm non-fiction, 260 fiction (190 studies, 18,573 participants); oral reading 183 wpm (77 studies, 5,965 participants). **VERIFIED (abstract)**. Reading along at 1x is roughly 40% slower than typical silent reading: a help on a hard day, a drag on an easy one.
- **No study has measured mind wandering while reading along.** We found none (search 2026-11-07); this matches the read-along post.
- **ADHD caution (match the ADHD post).** Moussaoui, Siddiqi, Cheung and Niemeier (2025), 76 university students: showing one word at a time in the centre of the screen improved comprehension for students with ADHD ("almost 13% relative to neurotypical controls"), while showing one word at a time in its usual place on the page, with the rest of the text hidden, did not. No audio. **VERIFIED** (Crossref abstract). This is not highlighting; don't call it that.

**Use in post:** "Possibly, but nobody has measured it. In a 2013 study of 211 students, listening alone produced the most mind wandering, so the audio on its own is not the fix. A 2023 meta-analysis of 30 studies found reading along gave only a small comprehension boost over reading alone, and only when the pace was set for the reader, which is what a narrator does. Our count of ten LibriVox recordings put narrators at a median 151 words a minute, against 238 to 260 for typical silent reading, so speed the narration up on easier days."

## 10. What the evidence does not show

1. **No study has measured mind wandering while reading along with an audiobook.** The case is indirect: listening alone drifts most, and paced reading along helps comprehension a little.
2. **Phone-presence effects are contested.** The 2017 "brain drain" result did not replicate directly; a 2024 meta-analysis of 33 studies found no effect (d = -0.02); another found only a smaller-than-claimed working memory effect. Notifications are better supported than mere presence.
3. **Attention span "shrinking" is not established.** The eight-second goldfish figure has no source. Mark's 47 seconds is how long office workers stay on one window, not attention capacity. The short-video meta-analysis is correlational.
4. **No trial on reading slumps** or any slump cure; no study of reading session length; no controlled study of finger or pacer reading for focus.
5. **Paper-versus-screen effects are small and task-dependent** (g about -0.2), mostly short expository texts read by students under test conditions, and not seen for narrative text.
6. **The reading decline is not shown to be caused by phones.** Bone 2025 says testing that was beyond its scope; Pew found book reading in any format flat since 2011; UK children's reading enjoyment rose in 2026.
7. **Reading aloud does not reliably cut mind wandering** (one study found less, one more); its proven benefit is memory for what was read aloud.
8. **The habit numbers are not about reading**, and the 66-day figure is an average from the 39 of 96 people whose data fitted well, within an 18-to-254-day range.
9. **Most mind-wandering studies are short lab sessions with undergraduates** and set passages, not adults reading novels at home.

## Claims to avoid (popular but unsupported)

- "Your attention span is 8 seconds, shorter than a goldfish." No traceable source (BBC 2017). Goldfish learn and remember well.
- "Attention spans have shrunk to 47 seconds." Say "office workers switched computer windows about every 47 seconds on average in Gloria Mark's studies".
- "It takes 23 minutes to get back on task after an interruption." Often attributed to Mark; not in the 2005 abstract and not verified here. Don't use.
- "Just having your phone nearby drains your brainpower." Contested: a failed direct replication and a null meta-analysis.
- "People mind wander 50% of the time while reading." Reading-specific probe rates in the studies above were about 36% to 42%; 30% to 50% is a general waking-life estimate.
- "Reading aloud stops mind wandering." One study found the opposite.
- "Reading on screens ruins comprehension." Small effect, mainly expository text under time pressure; none for narrative text.
- "It takes 21 days (or 66 days) to build a habit." 66 is an average from a subset, with an 18-to-254-day range; 21 has no basis in Lally.
- "Speed reading or a finger pacer will double your speed without losing comprehension." Rayner 2016 says unlikely.
- "Can't focus on reading means ADHD." ADHD symptoms must start before 12 and show in several settings; recent-onset trouble has many causes.
- "Most readers never finish books." Jellybooks data come from test readers of free ebooks; don't generalise to all readers.
- "Reading along fixes ADHD focus problems." Moussaoui found one word at a time in place did not help, and no audio was tested.
- "A 2007 study by Smallwood found...": Smallwood, Fishman and Schooler 2007 is a review with no sample.
