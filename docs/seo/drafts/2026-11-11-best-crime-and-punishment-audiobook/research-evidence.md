## (a) The facts most likely to shape the post

1. **Length, our own count.** Project Gutenberg #2554 (Constance Garnett's 1914 translation, the text all four free LibriVox recordings read) has **203,197 words** in the novel itself. At the site's median of 151 words a minute that is **22 h 26 min** at 1x, **17 h 57 min** at 1.25x and **14 h 57 min** at 1.5x. The Russian original (Wikisource text) has **170,747 words**, so Garnett's English runs about 19% longer.
2. **Chapters.** Six parts and an epilogue, **41 chapters** (7, 7, 6, 6, 5, 8, plus Epilogue I and II). The average chapter is 4,956 words, about **33 minutes** of listening; the shortest (Part IV, Ch. 6) is about 18 minutes, the longest (Part II, Ch. 6) about 50.
3. **Serialised in 8 issues, not 12.** The Russian Messenger printed it in 1866 issues No. 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 11 and 12, divided then into three parts and a conclusion; the book followed in 1867. English Wikipedia's "twelve monthly installments" is contradicted by the journal's contents list.
4. **Only two English translations are public domain in the US**: Frederick Whishaw's (1886) and Constance Garnett's (1914). At least 14 English translations exist. Penguin Classics has used three: Magarshack, McDuff and Oliver Ready.
5. **The Penguin Classics audiobook reads Oliver Ready, not McDuff.** Penguin's unabridged audio (2020, read by Don Warrington, 1,502 minutes) credits Ready as translator. The only audio on the McDuff Penguin Classics page is a 2005 *abridged* download read by Alex Jennings (315 minutes). This answers the "translated by david mcduff audiobook" query.
6. **Names are the listener's real difficulty, and translations differ.** The Russian uses Dunya *and* Dunechka, Sonya *and* Sonechka, Rodya *and* Rodka, Rodion Romanovich *and* the spoken "Romanych". Garnett folds these into one English form each (her "Dounia" 325 times ≈ Russian Dunya 209 + Dunechka 115). More than half of Garnett's words (55.4%) sit inside quotation marks, and the mother's letter alone runs 4,306 words, about 28 minutes.
7. **Critics on the translations, sourced.** Brodsky and Chukovsky on Garnett (via Remnick, New Yorker 2005); A. N. Wilson (Spectator 2014) and Boris Dralyuk (LARB 2015) for Ready, with the same passage compared across four translations; Gary Saul Morson's "Potemkin translations" against Pevear and Volokhonsky (via Wilson Quarterly 2010); Jennifer Wilson (The Nation 2018) on Katz vs Ready.
8. **Read or listen.** Listening and reading comprehension do not reliably differ overall (Clinton-Lisell 2022, 46 studies, g = 0.07), but minds wander most while listening (Varao Sousa 2013, 211 students), reading along adds little over self-paced reading (Clinton-Lisell 2023), and with novel excerpts learners understood least by listening alone (Hui & Godfroid 2026).

### 2. What critics and translators said (all VERIFIED unless marked)

**Garnett (1914)**
- Joseph Brodsky, quoted by David Remnick, "The Translation Wars", The New Yorker, November 7, 2005 issue: "The reason English-speaking readers can barely tell the difference between Tolstoy and Dostoevsky is that they aren't reading the prose of either one. They're reading Constance Garnett."
- Kornei Chukovsky, quoted by Remnick: Dostoevsky's style becomes under Garnett's pen "a safe blandscript: not a volcano, but a smooth lawn mowed in the English manner—which is to say a complete distortion of the original." (Jennifer Wilson in The Nation repeats the "smooth lawn" line.)
- Remnick himself: "She worked with such speed, with such an eye toward the finish line, that when she came across a word or a phrase that she couldn't make sense of she would skip it and move on." And on her output: "She translated seventy volumes of Russian prose for commercial publication, including all of Dostoyevsky's novels".
- Richard Pevear, quoted by Remnick: "Garnett breaks things into simple sentences, she Hemingwayizes Dostoyevsky, if you see what I mean."
- A. N. Wilson, The Spectator, 20 September 2014: "the first time you read it — perhaps in one of those old red Heinemann hardbacks — you did not notice how clumsy Garnett could be."
- In her favour (Remnick): "Without Garnett, the nineteenth-century 'Rooshians,' as Ezra Pound called them, would not have exerted such a rapid influence on the American literature of the early twentieth."

**Magarshack (Penguin)**
- A. N. Wilson (Spectator 2014) calls it "David Magarshack's (excellent) Penguin", but faults its "overturned tumbler" and "hatchet" in the murder scene (see section 3).
- UCL SSEES: his translations were considered "closer to Dostoevsky in literal sense" (quoted on the UCL page, attributed there to Cathy McAteer's research).

**Monas (1968, Signet)**
- Remnick, on Pevear and Volokhonsky's rule not to use English words that entered use after the novel: "In the Sidney Monas 'Crime and Punishment,' the translator uses 'pal' instead of something like 'old boy.'"

**McDuff (1991; revised 2003)**
- Penguin's own description (publisher claim, PRH): "This vivid translation by David McDuff has been acclaimed as the most accessible version of Dostoyevsky's great novel, rendering its dialogue with a unique force and naturalism."
- Boris Dralyuk (LARB 2015) uses McDuff as a comparison text (section 3).

**Pevear and Volokhonsky (1992)**
- Praise quoted by the publisher (PRH): Washington Post Book World, "The best [translation of Crime and Punishment] currently available"; Chicago Tribune, "The Pevear and Volokhonsky translation will become the standard English version."
- Gary Saul Morson, "The Pevearsion of Russian Literature", Commentary, July/August 2010, as quoted by the Wilson Quarterly digest "Potemkin Translators" (Fall 2010): P&V produce "Potemkin translations—apparently definitive but actually flat and fake on close inspection." The digest says Morson's case rests on their method (Volokhonsky's literal draft, Pevear's English) and on a *Dead Souls* example, not on Crime and Punishment. Commentary itself was blocked (403), so the essay was not read directly.
- Jennifer Wilson, "The World of 'Crime and Punishment'", The Nation, March 22, 2018: P&V's version has "prose so crisp and clear that reading Russian literature feels easy, perhaps easier than it should."
- Their method, per Remnick: "they will not use an English word that the Oxford English Dictionary says came into use after the publication of the novel they are translating."
- Sales then (Remnick, 2005): "'Crime and Punishment' twelve thousand" copies a year. A 2005 figure; do not present as current.

**Oliver Ready (2014)**
- A. N. Wilson, The Spectator, 20 September 2014, "This new translation of Crime and Punishment is a masterpiece": "this English version of Crime and Punishment really is better than, say, David Magarshack's (excellent) Penguin, or Constance Garnett's old Heinemann translation." Also: "Ready's version is colloquial, compellingly modern and — in so far as my amateurish knowledge of the language goes — much closer to the Russian." And: "Crime and Punishment, as well as being an horrific story and a compelling drama, is also extremely funny. Ready brings out this quality well".
- Boris Dralyuk, "All Is Permitted, All Over Again: Oliver Ready's Translation of Fyodor Dostoyevsky's 'Crime and Punishment'", Los Angeles Review of Books, October 18, 2015: praises "his ability to reproduce the whole heady brew of Dostoyevsky's novel in a consistent but nimble modern English".
- Times Literary Supplement, as quoted on Penguin's pages (blurb, TLS itself not read): "Oliver Ready's version is outstanding in finding le mot juste for all of Dostoevsky's graphic verbs and odd objects".
- Jennifer Wilson (The Nation 2018): "Ready's more elegant British rendering" (against Katz's "American simplicity and informality").
- Academic review exists: Connor Doak, "Review of: Fyodor Dostoevsky, Crime and Punishment. Translated by Oliver Ready.", Slavic and East European Journal 59(2), 298-300, 2015 (University of Bristol research record, VERIFIED as a record; its text was not read).
- PEN Translation Prize 2016 shortlist: search summary only, UNVERIFIED.

**Michael R. Katz (2017)**
- Steve Donoghue, Open Letters Monthly, as quoted by Middlebury (November 3, 2017): "the best, truest translation of Dostoevsky's masterpiece into English."
- Jennifer Wilson (The Nation 2018): Katz aims to bring out what he calls the novel's "richness of registers or tones"; his version "has an American simplicity and informality that sets it apart from Ready's more elegant British rendering".

### 3. The same passage in several translations (from named reviewers; in-copyright quotes kept to one line each)

**a) The horse-beating dream, Part I Ch. 5: the father's "пьяные, шалят, дураки"** (Russian quoted by Dralyuk; LARB 2015, VERIFIED):
- Garnett (1914): "They are drunken and foolish, they are in fun" (also VERIFIED in Gutenberg #2554)
- McDuff (1991): "They're drunk, playing mischief, the fools"
- Pevear and Volokhonsky (1992): "They're drunk, they're playing pranks, the fools"
- Ready (2014): "Just drunken idiots fooling around"
- Dralyuk's verdict: Ready's line "sounds far fresher and produces a far more poignant effect than previous efforts"; "'They are in fun,' is, of course, hopelessly dated".

**b) Same scene, the mare's collapse** (LARB 2015; Russian "лом снова со всего размаху ложится ей на спину, и она падает на землю, точно ей подсекли все четыре ноги разом"):
- Garnett: "the bar fell again with a swinging blow on her back and she fell on the ground like a log" (VERIFIED in Gutenberg)
- McDuff: "the crowbar again came down on her back, and she fell to the ground, as if all her four legs had been cut away from under her at once"
- Pevear and Volokhonsky: "another full swing of the crowbar lands on her back, and she falls to the ground as if all four legs had been cut from under her"

What the table shows (OUR OBSERVATION from the counts and a spot check):
- The Russian gives most main characters three or four names: surname, first name plus patronymic, a short form, and a pet or rough form. Raskolnikov is "Raskolnikov", "Rodion Romanovich", "Rodion Romanych", "Rodya" and "Rodka".
- Garnett merges forms. Her "Dounia" (325) matches Russian Dunya + Dunechka (209 + 115 = 324); her "Sonia" (402) matches Sonya + Sonechka (379 + 21 = 400); her "Rodion Romanovitch" (86) matches the full plus spoken patronymic (39 + 51 = 90). Spot check: Razumikhin's "а я товарищ Родькин" is Garnett's "I am a comrade of Rodya's" (both VERIFIED in the two texts). So a listener to the free (Garnett) recordings hears fewer name forms than a Russian reader sees.
- Spellings change by translation: Garnett "Dounia", "Sonia", "Razumihin", "Romanovitch", "Svidrigaïlov"; Alma's Cockrell description uses "Rodion Romanovich Raskolnikov", "Dunya" and "Svidrigailov"; Penguin's McDuff and Ready descriptions both use "Sonya"; Dralyuk (LARB) writes "Razumikhin" and "Porfiry Petrovich" (VERIFIED as those pages print them).
- Alma's edition includes a "List of Characters" and "Table of Ranks" (VERIFIED, Alma page). Whether other editions include a character list was not checked.

Other listener difficulties, from our data: chapters average about 33 minutes; 55.4% of the words are quoted speech, letters or thoughts, so one narrator voices many people; the mother's letter runs about 28 minutes unbroken. The sentences themselves are short in Garnett (Gutenberg: reading ease 83.0, "6th grade"; our mean 12.7 words per sentence).

## (e) Read or listen: the evidence (6 studies, all Crossref- or ERIC-checked today)

**1. Clinton-Lisell (2022), "Listening Ears or Reading Eyes: A Meta-Analysis of Reading and Listening Comprehension Comparisons", Review of Educational Research 92(4), 543-582 (online 29 Dec 2021). DOI 10.3102/00346543211060871.** VERIFIED (Crossref title; abstract via OpenAlex).
- "Based on robust variance estimation (46 studies; N = 4,687), the overall difference between reading and listening comprehension was not reliably different (g = 0.07, p = .23)."
- "Reading was beneficial over listening when the reading condition was self-paced (g = 0.13, p = .049)". Reading also helped for inferential questions (g = 0.36, p = .02) but not literal ones (g = -0.01).
- Sentence: "A 2022 meta-analysis of 46 studies with 4,687 people found no reliable overall difference between understanding a text by reading it and by listening to it, though reading did better on questions that need inference."

**2. Varao Sousa, Carriere and Smilek (2013), "The way we encounter reading material influences how frequently we mind wander", Frontiers in Psychology 4. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00892.** VERIFIED (Crossref title; abstract via OpenAlex; full text via Europe PMC PMC3842750).
- Full text: "A total of 235 University of Waterloo students participated ... A total of 24 participants ... were dropped", leaving 211.
- Abstract: "reading aloud led to the least amount of mind wandering, while listening to the passage led to the most mind wandering. Listening to the passage was also associated with the poorest memory performance and the least interest in the material."
- The proportions used on the site before (0.51 listening vs 0.36 silent reading) are in the paper's figure; they were verified in earlier site research (2026-11-01) and not re-read today.

**3. Clinton-Lisell (2023), "Does Reading While Listening to Text Improve Comprehension Compared to Reading Only? A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis", Educational Research: Theory and Practice. ERIC EJ1403866 (no journal DOI; preprint DOI 10.31219/osf.io/ku6tv, Crossref title "Reading while listening meta-analysis").** VERIFIED (ERIC API abstract; Crossref for the preprint).
- "30 eligible studies (total N = 1945) with 62 effect sizes"; "a trivial overall benefit of reading while listening over reading only on comprehension was found g = 0.18"; experimenter-paced "g = 0.41"; "There were no reliable effects of reading while listening when reading was self-paced, g = 0.06".

**4. Hui and Godfroid (2026; online 12 June 2025), "Listening, Reading, or Both? Rethinking the Comprehension Benefits of Reading-While-Listening", Language Learning 76(1), 311-351. DOI 10.1111/lang.12721.** VERIFIED (Crossref title, volume, pages; abstract via OpenAlex).
- "eighty-six intermediate-to-advanced Chinese learners of English read, read and listened, and listened to different excerpts of a novel. Contrary to our preregistered hypotheses, participants comprehended the text less well when reading-while-listening than when reading it silently. Both reading conditions yielded better comprehension than listening-only."
- Year note: Crossref "issued" is 2025 (online); the journal issue is 2026. The 2026-11-09 and 2026-11-10 posts used 2026.

**5. Richardson, Griffin, Zaki, Stephenson, Yan, Curry, Noble, Hogan, Skipper and Devlin (2020), "Engagement in video and audio narratives: contrasting self-report and physiological measures", Scientific Reports 10, 11298. DOI 10.1038/s41598-020-68253-2.** VERIFIED (Crossref title and abstract; full text via Europe PMC PMC7347852). New to the site.
- Method: "109 participants were recruited from UCL's subject pool ... Of the 102 (41 M, 61F) who completed the experiment, their ages ranged from 18 to 55". "Participants experienced the same set of eight scenes from fictional stories. For each story, participants either heard a passage from an audiobook, or saw a TV or movie adaptation of the same scene." Books included Pride and Prejudice, Great Expectations, The Hound of the Baskervilles and The Silence of the Lambs.
- Result: "Although participants self-reported greater involvement for watching video relative to listening to auditory scenes, stronger physiological responses were recorded for auditory stories", with "higher and more variable heart rates, greater electrodermal activity, and even higher body temperatures."
- Sentence: "In a 2020 study of 102 adults, Richardson and colleagues found that audiobook scenes from novels such as Great Expectations raised heart rate and skin conductance more than the film versions of the same scenes, even though people said the films felt more involving."
- Limits: short scenes, audio vs video (not audio vs print).

**6. Rodero and Lucas (2023; online 28 June 2021), "Synthetic versus human voices in audiobooks: The human emotional intimacy effect", New Media & Society 25(7), 1746-1764. DOI 10.1177/14614448211024142.** VERIFIED (Crossref title, volume, pages; abstract via OpenAlex).
- "listeners enjoyed stories narrated by a human voice more than a synthetic one. Also, they created more mental images, were more engaged, paid more attention, had a more positive emotional response, and remembered more information."
- Sample size is not in the abstract (UNVERIFIED); do not give one.

**Speed note (already cited on the site, for the 1.25x and 1.5x figures): Murphy, Hoover, Agadzhanyan, Kuehn and Castel (2022; online 22 Nov 2021), "Learning in double time: The effect of lecture video speed on immediate and delayed comprehension", Applied Cognitive Psychology 36(1), 69-82. DOI 10.1002/acp.3899.** VERIFIED (Crossref; abstract via OpenAlex): "minimal costs incurred by increasing video speed from 1x to 1.5x, or 2x speed, but performance declined beyond 2x speed." Lecture videos, not novels; the abstract itself warns "these trends may differ for videos with different speech rates, complexity or difficulty".

**What the evidence does not show:**
- No study tests listening to Crime and Punishment, a Russian novel, or any book-length text start to finish; all use passages, chapters or excerpts.
- No study measures how listeners keep track of character names, or whether a character list or read-along text helps with names.
- Hui and Godfroid's participants were second-language learners; Clinton-Lisell's meta-analyses pool all ages. Neither isolates adult native-English readers of long fiction.
- Richardson 2020 compares audio with video, not with print.
- No study compares one translation's comprehensibility with another's in audio.

## Do not claim

1. That Crime and Punishment ran in "twelve monthly installments". It appeared in 8 issues of The Russian Messenger in 1866 (No. 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 11, 12).
2. That the magazine version had six parts, or that the six-part structure dates exactly from 1867. The magazine printed three parts and a conclusion; no page read says when the six-part division arrived.
3. That the first English translation appeared in 1885, or name its publisher as fact. UCL says 1886 (Frederick Whishaw); Vizetelly is unconfirmed.
4. Exact first-publication years or publishers for Magarshack (1951), Kropotkin (1953), Coulson (1953, Oxford), Scammell (1963), Katzer (1985, Raduga) or Cockrell (2022). Only Wikipedia gives them. McDuff's 1991 is safe only as "1991" (LARB), not "Viking 1991".
5. That any translation after Garnett's is public domain in the US. Only Whishaw (1886) and Garnett (1914) are.
