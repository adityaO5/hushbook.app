Post: "Best Audiobook Version of War and Peace: Every Translation and Narrator Compared" (slug `best-war-and-peace-audiobook`).

Scope of this file: the book (publication, structure, word counts, listening time), every English translation (dates, publishers, copyright, what critics said, the same passage in several versions), the French, what makes the novel hard for listeners (cast and names), and the read-or-listen studies. ## (a) The facts most likely to shape the post

1. **Length, our own count.** Project Gutenberg #2600 (the Louise and Aylmer Maude translation, the text both Maude LibriVox sets read) has **562,416 words** in the novel itself by the sibling post's counting rules. The site already publishes **561,870** for the same text (`/blog/how-long-is-an-audiobook`); the two differ by 0.1%, so keep 561,870 in prose. At the site's median of 151 words a minute that is **about 62 hours at 1x, 50 at 1.25x, 41 at 1.5x and 31 at 2x**. LibriVox's solo Maude recording runs 62:27:13, which implies 150 words a minute, matching the site.
2. **Days to finish (151 wpm):** at 30 minutes a day, 125 days at 1x and 63 days at 2x; at 60 minutes a day, 63 days at 1x and 32 at 2x; at 90 minutes a day, 42 days at 1x and 21 at 2x. Full grid in (b)2.
3. **361 or 365 chapters: the Russian has 361, the Maude text has 365.** The task brief had this the other way round. Russian editions (ilibrary.ru contents, the Jubilee edition on Wikisource) give 4 volumes, 15 parts and a two-part epilogue, 361 chapters. The Maude text on Gutenberg has 15 Books and two epilogues, 365 chapters, because it splits two long Russian chapters in Book One into five (Russian Vol. 1, Part 1, Ch. IV and VI) and turns the opening paragraphs of Russian Vol. 2, Part 3, Ch. I into a separate 146-word chapter at the end of Book Five. LibriVox's solo Maude set has 365 sections, one per Maude chapter. So chapter numbers in a Maude recording do not match a Russian-numbered edition after Book One, Chapter 3.
4. **The Russian original has 448,184 words** (our count, all 361 chapters, ilibrary.ru text). Maude's English runs about 25% longer. **3.4% of the Russian words are in Latin script** (mostly French, plus some German and Latin), but the French is front-loaded: **11.0% of Vol. 1, Part 1** (the first five hours of listening) against 2.7% in the rest. Orlando Figes and Richard Pevear put the French at "about 2 percent"; Russian Wikipedia gives 2.52% from a 1978 Tula study. Our figure counts every Latin-script word, so treat "2 to 3 percent" as the safe range.
5. **What each translation does with the French, for a listener.** Pevear and Volokhonsky (2007) keep all of it, with English in footnotes; Mandelker's 2010 revision of Maude restores it; Leo Wiener (1904) kept the opening speech in French; Garnett and the Maudes translate it (the Maude text keeps 136 short foreign phrases with footnotes, by our count); Edmonds kept "Eh bien, mon prince" and put the rest of the opening speech in English; Briggs (2005) translates all of it; Bromfield's 2007 "original version" has none. A recording of a P&V or Mandelker text therefore has a narrator speaking French at length in the first hours; a Maude, Garnett or Briggs recording does not.
6. **Cast size, sourced carefully.** Pevear (via Remnick, 2005): "The novel has five hundred historical figures and fictional figures". Russian Wikipedia: "from 559 to 570" characters, citing S. I. Zinin (1978); the popular "559 characters, about 200 historical" (Mental Floss, Russian Life) has no cited source. The figure "580" was not found on any page read. Safe wording: "more than 500 named characters".
7. **Names in the Maude text are anglicised and sometimes shared.** Maude writes Prince Andrew (1,072 times), Nicholas (632), Princess Mary (512), where P&V write Andrei, Nikolai, Marya (Wood, Herrmann). In Maude, "Nicholas" is three people: Nicholas Rostov, old Prince Nicholas Bolkonski ("Prince Nicholas" 26 times) and Andrew's son ("little Nicholas" 25 times). The Russian separates them (Николай, Николай Андреич, Николушка). The French name forms in the Russian (André 30, Nicolas 41, Marie 20) almost vanish in Maude (André 2, Nicolas 0, Marie 8). Mandelker's revision removes the anglicised names (OUP blurb).
8. **Copyright.** Five English translations are public domain in the US: Clara Bell (1885 to 1886, made from a French translation), Nathan Haskell Dole (1889), Leo Wiener (1904), Constance Garnett (1904), Louise and Aylmer Maude (1922 to 1923). Everything from Edmonds (1957) on is in copyright. The only complete War and Peace on Project Gutenberg is the Maude (#2600).
9. **Tolstoy's praise of the Maudes is real but is not about War and Peace.** Aylmer Maude quoted Tolstoy in a letter to The Saturday Review dated 1 January 1905: "Better translators both for knowledge of the two languages and for penetration into the very meaning of the matter translated could not be invented". Tolstoy died in 1910; the Maudes' War and Peace appeared in 1922 to 1923. And it is Maude quoting Tolstoy, not a letter from Tolstoy we have read.
10. **Read or listen.** Listening and reading comprehension do not reliably differ overall (Clinton-Lisell 2022, 46 studies, g = 0.07), but minds wander most when listening alone (Varao Sousa 2013; Kopp and D'Mello 2016, with The Red-Headed League and Walden), and with a whole book chapter adults scored the same by audiobook, e-text or both (Rogowsky 2016). No study tests a 60-hour novel.

Why 365 and 361 (OUR OBSERVATION from `wp-chapter-align.py`, each split checked by first sentence):
- **Book One (+3).** Russian Vol. 1, Part 1, Ch. IV (2,271 words) is Maude's Ch. 4 ("Just then another visitor entered the drawing room") and Ch. 5 ("And what do you think of this latest comedy"). Russian Ch. VI (3,451 words, the longest Russian chapter, opening "В соседней комнате зашумело женское платье") is Maude's Ch. 7 ("The rustle of a woman's dress was heard in the next room"), Ch. 8 ("The friends were silent") and Ch. 9 ("It was past one o'clock when Pierre left his friend"). From there on Maude's numbers run three ahead.
- **Book Five (+1).** Russian Vol. 2, Part 3, Ch. I opens "В 1808-м году император Александр ездил в Эрфурт". Maude puts that sentence at the end of Book Five, Ch. 21 ("In 1808 the Emperor Alexander went to Erfurt...") and the next paragraphs as Book Five, Ch. 22 ("In 1809 the intimacy between 'the world's two arbiters'...", 146 words, the shortest chapter in the Maude text). Book Six then starts at "Prince Andrew had spent two years continuously in the country."
- Every other book has the same chapter count as its Russian part.
- Why the Maudes divided it this way (an earlier Russian edition's division, or their own choice) was not found on any page read: UNVERIFIED. English Wikipedia's "consisting of 361 chapters" refers to the Russian.

### 2. What critics and translators said (all VERIFIED unless marked)

**Garnett (1904)**
- Remnick, "The Translation Wars", The New Yorker, November 7, 2005 issue: Garnett "turned down an offer from Tolstoy's close friends Louise and Aylmer Maude to collaborate on a translation of 'War and Peace' and did it on her own. (So, too, did the Maudes, her only rival in Tolstoy.) Garnett went nearly blind working on 'War and Peace.'"
- Remnick on Hemingway: he "could never get through 'War and Peace'—not 'until I got the Constance Garnett translation.'"
- Figes (NYRB 2007): she "translated into graceful late-Victorian prose seventy major Russian works"; "Most translators have followed Garnett in cutting all but a few words of the French". Garnett breaks the long Rastopchin sentence "into seven sentences".
- James Wood ("Movable Types", The New Yorker, November 26, 2007 issue): Garnett, Edmonds and the Maudes "have tended to be somewhat activist, sidestepping difficult words, smoothing the rhythm of the Russian, and eliminating one of Tolstoy's most distinctive elements, repetition."
- Zoja Pavlovskis-Petit (Encyclopedia of Literary Translation into English, 2000), as quoted by English Wikipedia: Garnett's War and Peace "is frequently inexact and contains too many anglicisms." UNVERIFIED at source.

**Louise and Aylmer Maude (1922–23)**
- Tolstoy, as quoted by Aylmer Maude in "The Translating of Russian", a letter dated "Great Baddow, Chelmsford, 1 January, 1905", The Saturday Review No. 2,567, Vol. 99, 7 January 1905, p. 17: "Better translators both for knowledge of the two languages and for penetration into the very meaning of the matter translated could not be invented". Maude was defending their translation of Tolstoy's play The Power of Darkness. VERIFIED (archive.org scan of the 1905 volume). This praises the Maudes as translators in general, 17 years before their War and Peace.
- Herrmann (Claremont Review of Books, Summer 2008): the Maudes "were disciples of Tolstoy"; "Tolstoy authorized Aylmer to write his biography and to translate his works into English. Louise translated much of the fiction, including War and Peace and Resurrection"; "Their faithful translation, which the Maudes revised in 1933, remains available in a Norton Critical Edition".
- Wood (2007): in the oak scene "The Maudes use 'sappy,' too, but drop the third iteration"; their "Neither gnarled fingers nor old scars nor old doubts and sorrows were any of them in evidence now" "sounds like an English lyric poet—Housman, perhaps."
- Figes (2007): in the scene at Prince Andrei's coffin, "Louise and Aylmer Maude say both words three times each, omitting one verb altogether"; "The Maude translation uses three different words for 'anteroom' and omits it once."
- OUP blurb for the 2010 revision (publisher claim): "The Maudes' translation of Tolstoy's epic masterpiece has long been considered the best English version".
- Pavlovskis-Petit via English Wikipedia: "this should have been the best translation, but the Maudes' lack of adroitness in dealing with Russian folk idiom, and their style in general, place this version below Dunnigan's." UNVERIFIED at source.

**Edmonds (1957)**
- Figes: "Rosemary Edmonds (who follows Garnett closely)".
- Herrmann: "many are still devoted to a Penguin translation by Rosemary Edmonds, since superseded by Briggs."
- Pavlovskis-Petit via English Wikipedia: "the work of a sound scholar but not the best possible translator ... a respectable translation but not on the level of Dunnigan or Maude." UNVERIFIED at source.

**Dunnigan (1968)**
- Pavlovskis-Petit via English Wikipedia: "Of all the translations of War and Peace, Dunnigan's (1968) is the best... Unlike the other translators, Dunnigan even succeeds with many characteristically Russian folk expressions and proverbs". UNVERIFIED at source. No other review read.

**Briggs (2005)**
- Briggs, quoted by Remnick from the Daily Telegraph: "Professional translators are generally mediocre people like me, not great poetic geniuses."
- Remnick on a TLS article: Briggs "rendered all Tolstoy's French into English and even spelled out some of General Kutuzov's obscenities. (Tolstoy had obscured the profanity with ellipses.)" Edmonds: "It serves them right, the b—— b——s!"; Briggs: "They asked for it, the fucking bastards!"
- Briggs's own words, quoted by Herrmann (CRB 2008): "Language changes, and, without worshiping modernity for its own sake, publishers recognize the need to accommodate new readers by using phrasing more closely attuned to their way of speaking." And: "Tolstoy's literary style has its faults, such as undue repetition, grammatical inaccuracy and some sentences of excessive length."
- Briggs's afterword, quoted by Michael Wood (LRB, 22 May 2008): try "to imagine how the average Russian reads War and Peace and . . . try to recapture something similar in the translated text".
- Herrmann: "an unabashed cavalier"; "a fluid, enjoyable, colloquial—and definitely modern—British version."
- Michael Wood (LRB): "Surely Briggs's 'what was left of the prince' is better than Pevear and Volokhonsky's 'what had been he'?"
- Publisher praise (PRH page): Robert A. Maguire, "The best translation so far of Tolstoy's masterpiece into English"; John Bayley, "a particular exactness and a vigorous precision not to be found, I think, in any previous translation". Penguin UK: Antony Beevor, "A masterpiece ... This new translation is excellent".
- Briggs's preface on soldiers' speech specifically: **not found on any page read. UNVERIFIED.** Reviewers' remarks about British slang in soldiers' dialogue were seen only in search summaries and blogs.
- Note: Orlando Figes wrote the afterword to the Penguin Briggs edition (Penguin UK, PRH) and then praised Pevear and Volokhonsky in the NYRB.

**Pevear and Volokhonsky (2007)**
- Figes (NYRB): "the excellent new translation by Richard Pevear and Larissa Volokhonsky"; "What really makes this wonderfully fresh and readable translation stand out from its predecessors is its absolute fidelity to the language of Tolstoy." Also notes "occasional misjudgments" and "one or two errors".
- Wood (New Yorker): the translation "brings us their palpability as perhaps never before"; "gives us new access to the spirit and order of the book."
- Herrmann (CRB): "The result is as close to Tolstoy's original as one can get without reading Russian."
- Michael Wood (LRB): "In what world of English does a man address another as 'my gentle'?"
- Janet Malcolm, letter, LRB Vol. 30 No. 16, 14 August 2008: "Pevear has promoted his and Larissa Volokhonsky's wooden translation of War and Peace by claiming that it offers an exquisite rendering of Tolstoy's own tin ear. Isn't it time to examine – rather than to swallow – this claim?"
- Gary Saul Morson's "Potemkin translations" critique (Commentary 2010, via the Wilson Quarterly digest, see the Crime and Punishment research file) is about P&V in general; the digest's example is Dead Souls. **No War and Peace-specific statement by Morson was verified**, and the "Maude revised by Mandelker" recommendation that turns up in searches comes from a blog commenter, not from Morson.

**Bromfield (2007), the "Original Version"**
- Figes (footnote 8): "the misleadingly described 'original version' of War and Peace (Ecco, 2007), which is in fact a version of an early draft, based on a text prepared by the Tolstoy scholar Evelina Zaidenshnur, and much shorter than the first published edition".
- NPR (Lynn Neary, October 22, 2007): "some 400 pages shorter"; Knopf editor LuAnn Walther: "They wouldn't get War and Peace ... They're two different books."; Ecco publisher Daniel Halpern: it is "the first version that Tolstoy wrote and signed 'The End' to", and "it's got a different ending."
- Mail & Guardian (2007-10-22): "runs to just 886 pages, does away with the French and the philosophical digressions and boasts a happy ending."
- Kirkus: "It reproduces the 1866 text: a leaner version of the novel, written before Tolstoy had conceived the discursive chapters of historical argument".
- Russian Life (Eugenia Sokolskaya, July 1, 2015): the 1866 manuscript "presented French conversations in Russian (the author's translation) instead of in French".

**Mandelker (2010)**
- OUP description (via bookseller): "now for the first time it has been revised to bring it fully into line with modern approaches to the text. French passages are restored, Anglicization of Russian names removed, and outmoded expressions updated." No independent review read.

**Wiener (1904), Dole (1889), Bell (1885–86)**
- No critical verdict read. Facts only: Bell translated from a French version "BY A RUSSIAN LADY"; Dole "AUTHORIZED TRANSLATION", footnotes for some "polyglot conversation"; Wiener "Translated from the Original Russian", kept the French opening.

### 3. The same passage in several translations (from named critics; in-copyright translations one line each)

**a) Old Prince Bolkonsky moves to Moscow** (Russian Vol. 2, Part 5, Ch. II; Maude Book Eight, Ch. 2). Side by side in Lesley Herrmann, "Puritans and Cavaliers", Claremont Review of Books, Summer 2008:
- Russian: "В начале зимы, князь Николай Андреич Болконский с дочерью приехали в Москву." (Wikisource Jubilee text, VERIFIED)
- Pevear and Volokhonsky: "At the beginning of winter, Prince Nikolai Andreich Bolkonsky came to Moscow with his daughter."
- Maude: "At the beginning of winter Prince Nicholas Bolkónski and his daughter moved to Moscow." (also VERIFIED in Gutenberg #2600)
- Garnett: "At the beginning of the winter Prince Nikolay Andreitch Bolkonsky and his daughter moved to Moscow."
- Briggs: "At the beginning of the winter old Prince Bolkonsky and his daughter moved to Moscow."
- Herrmann: "All four translations get the point across, and each has its strengths. Pevear-Volokhonsky, by keeping Tolstoy's clauses intact, are the most accurate." She notes Briggs calls him "old Prince Bolkonsky" "to avoid using the prince's name and patronymic, a problem that flummoxes so many first-time readers in English."
- This one sentence shows four ways of naming one man: Nikolai Andreich, Nicholas, Nikolay Andreitch, "old Prince Bolkonsky". Useful for the names section.

**b) The oak, Russian Vol. 2, Part 3, Ch. III** (James Wood, New Yorker 2007):
- Russian: "Целый день был жаркий, где-то собиралась гроза, но только небольшая тучка брызнула на пыль дороги и на сочные листья." (Wikisource, VERIFIED)
- Garnett: "The whole day had been hot; a storm was gathering, but only a small rain-cloud had sprinkled the dust of the road and the sappy leaves."
- Maude: "The whole day had been hot. Somewhere a storm was gathering, but only a small cloud had scattered some raindrops lightly, sprinkling the road and the sappy leaves." (Gutenberg, VERIFIED)
- Pevear and Volokhonsky: "The whole day had been hot, there was a thunderstorm gathering somewhere, but only a small cloud had sent a sprinkle over the dust of the road and the juicy leaves."
- Briggs: renders сочные as "lush", "uses it twice, and then substitutes 'succulent.'" (Wood; Briggs's sentence not quoted)

**c) The old prince's body** (Michael Wood, LRB 2008, and the letters that followed):
- Pevear and Volokhonsky: "the women washed what had been he"
- Briggs: "what was left of the prince" (Wood) or "what was left of him" (Pevear's letter, 19 June 2008)
- Garnett and Maude: "the women washed what had been the prince" (Janet Malcolm's letter; Maude also VERIFIED in Gutenberg)

**d) The children in the First Epilogue** (Wood 2007): Tolstoy's line, per Pevear and Volokhonsky, "The children were riding to Moscow on chairs and invited her to come with them." Maude: "The children were playing at 'going to Moscow' in a carriage made of chairs and invited her to go with them." (VERIFIED in Gutenberg). Wood: "Garnett, the Maudes, and Briggs all insert an explanatory 'playing at'".

**e) Moscow workmen before the fall of the city** (Figes 2007): Pevear and Volokhonsky, "He ought to square it properly with people!"; Edmonds, "Why don't he give us our wages we're entitled to?"; Maude (Gutenberg, VERIFIED), "He should pay folks off properly". Figes: earlier versions are "too cockney-coy, too grammatically correct, to communicate the drunken street-talk".

## (f) Read or listen: the evidence (8 studies, all Crossref- or ERIC-checked today with `wp-crossref-check.py`)

**1. Clinton-Lisell (2022), "Listening Ears or Reading Eyes: A Meta-Analysis of Reading and Listening Comprehension Comparisons", Review of Educational Research 92(4), 543-582 (online 29 Dec 2021). DOI 10.3102/00346543211060871.** VERIFIED (Crossref title, volume, pages; abstract via OpenAlex).
- "Based on robust variance estimation (46 studies; N = 4,687), the overall difference between reading and listening comprehension was not reliably different (g = 0.07, p = .23)."
- "Reading was beneficial over listening when the reading condition was self-paced (g = 0.13, p = .049)". Reading also helped for inferential questions (g = 0.36, p = .02) but not literal ones (g = -0.01).

**2. Varao Sousa, Carriere and Smilek (2013), "The way we encounter reading material influences how frequently we mind wander", Frontiers in Psychology 4. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00892.** VERIFIED (Crossref title; abstract via OpenAlex).
- "reading aloud led to the least amount of mind wandering, while listening to the passage led to the most mind wandering. Listening to the passage was also associated with the poorest memory performance and the least interest in the material."
- 211 analysed students (235 minus 24) per the sibling file's reading of the full text (Europe PMC PMC3842750); not re-read today.

**3. Clinton-Lisell (2023), "Does Reading While Listening to Text Improve Comprehension Compared to Reading Only? A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis", Educational Research: Theory and Practice. ERIC EJ1403866 (preprint DOI 10.31219/osf.io/ku6tv, Crossref title "Reading while listening meta-analysis").** VERIFIED (ERIC API; Crossref).
- "30 eligible studies (total N = 1945) with 62 effect sizes"; "a trivial overall benefit of reading while listening over reading only on comprehension was found g = 0.18"; experimenter-paced "g = 0.41"; "There were no reliable effects of reading while listening when reading was self-paced, g = 0.06".

- "eighty-six intermediate-to-advanced Chinese learners of English read, read and listened, and listened to different excerpts of a novel. Contrary to our preregistered hypotheses, participants comprehended the text less well when reading-while-listening than when reading it silently. Both reading conditions yielded better comprehension than listening-only."

**5. DOI 10.1038/s41598-020-68253-2.** VERIFIED (Crossref; abstract via OpenAlex).
- "Although participants self-reported greater involvement for watching video relative to listening to auditory scenes, stronger physiological responses were recorded for auditory stories."
- 102 completers and the book list (Pride and Prejudice, Great Expectations and others) come from the sibling file's full-text reading; not re-read today. Audio vs video, not audio vs print.

- Texts: "The Red Headed League (Experiment 1 and 2) and Walden (Experiment 3)". "An Audio only condition produced the most mind wandering. Two conditions that presumably consumed more executive resources than the Audio Only condition (i.e. Audio + Text and Self‐paced Reading) produced equivalent amounts of mind wandering during Experiments 1 and 2."
- Why it fits: literary texts, and it is the one study here with an audio-plus-text condition against audio alone: following the text while listening cut mind wandering compared with listening only.
- Sample sizes are not in the abstract: UNVERIFIED; do not give one.

**8. NEW to this post (already on the site): Rogowsky, Calhoun and Tallal (2016), "Does Modality Matter? The Effects of Reading, Listening, and Dual Modality on Comprehension", SAGE Open 6(3). DOI 10.1177/2158244016669550.** VERIFIED (Crossref; abstract via OpenAlex).
- "Ninety-one participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups that received the same instructional material (the preface and a chapter from a non-fiction book), but each in a different input modality (digital audiobook, e-text, dual modality)." "No statistically significant differences were found ... at Time 1 or Time 2" (2-week retention).
- Why it fits: the longest material of any study here (a whole chapter plus preface), tested again after two weeks. Still nowhere near 62 hours. Already cited in `does-reading-along-with-an-audiobook-help`, `how-to-retain-information-from-audiobooks` and others.

**Speed note (already on the site): Murphy, Hoover, Agadzhanyan, Kuehn and Castel (2022; online 22 Nov 2021), "Learning in double time: The effect of lecture video speed on immediate and delayed comprehension", Applied Cognitive Psychology 36(1), 69-82. DOI 10.1002/acp.3899.** VERIFIED: "minimal costs incurred by increasing video speed from 1x to 1.5x, or 2x speed, but performance declined beyond 2x speed." Lecture videos; "these trends may differ for videos with different speech rates, complexity or difficulty".

**For the hard-to-read section, link rather than re-cite:** Feng, D'Mello and Graesser (2013), "Mind wandering while reading easy and difficult texts", Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 20(3), 586-592, DOI 10.3758/s13423-012-0367-y (title VERIFIED in Crossref today; abstract not available in OpenAlex), is already used in `/blog/how-to-read-classics-without-getting-bored`. Link that post instead of adding a ninth study.

**What the evidence does not show:**
- No study tests listening to War and Peace, a Russian novel, or any text longer than a book chapter; nothing covers a 60-hour listen spread over weeks.
- No study measures how listeners keep track of 500 characters or of several name forms per person, or whether a character list or read-along text helps with names.
- No study compares listening to French-in-the-text with listening to an all-English translation.
- Kopp and D'Mello's audio-plus-text finding is about mind wandering, not comprehension; Clinton-Lisell 2023 found no reliable comprehension benefit of reading along for self-paced readers, and Hui and Godfroid found reading along worse than silent reading for second-language learners.
- Murphy et al. used lecture videos, not novels.

## (h) Conflicts between sources

1. **Chapter count, brief vs texts:** the brief said "361 in Maude vs 365 in others". The texts say the reverse: Maude (Gutenberg #2600) has 365, the Russian has 361 (ilibrary.ru contents; English Wikipedia "361 chapters").
2. **Serialisation years:** English Wikipedia "from 1865 to 1867" vs Russian Wikipedia "1865—1866" and Wikisource (1866 issues No. 2 to 4; nothing by L. N. Tolstoy in the 1867 contents). Use "1865 and 1866".
3. **Wikisource Russian text incomplete:** 360 chapters, ends inside Vol. 3, Part 3, Ch. XXXIII. Use the ilibrary.ru count (361 chapters, 448,184 words).
4. **Share of French:** Figes "about 2 percent", Pevear "2 percent", Herrmann "2%", Russian Wikipedia 2.52% (Shcherbak-Maimeskul 1978), our Latin-script count 3.42%. Different methods; ours includes German, Latin and every Latin-letter token. Say "about 2 to 3 percent" or quote Figes and give ours as a separate count.
5. **"First and only" to keep the French:** Figes says P&V "are the first and only ones to retain all the French"; Wiener's 1904 translation keeps the opening speech in French (scan). Wiener may not keep all of it (not checked). Do not repeat "first".
6. **Figes's "all but seven words":** our reading of the opening speech finds 10 Russian words. Do not repeat the number.
7. **Character count:** Pevear ~500 ("the vast majority" historical), Mail & Guardian "500 or so", Russian Wikipedia 559 to 570 (Zinin 1978), Mental Floss and Russian Life 559 with "about 200" historical; "580" unfound.
8. **Briggs year:** UK Penguin Classics 2005 (Remnick, Wood, Michael Wood) vs US "Viking, 2006" (Figes) and US paperback Nov 28, 2006 (PRH). Use 2005.
9. **P&V page count:** 1,273 (Knopf per Figes; Vintage UK per LRB), 1,267 (Mail & Guardian), 1,296 (Moscow Times; 2008 Vintage paperback per PRH's Brightly shop). Formats differ.
10. **Bromfield length:** "some 400 pages shorter" (NPR), 886 pages (Mail & Guardian), 912 (Moscow Times). And the Zakharov slogan in four renderings: "Half the usual length. Less war and more peace. No philosophical digressions or incomprehensible French. A happy ending" (Figes), "twice as short, four times as interesting … more peace, less war" (Mail & Guardian, which also calls Ecco the "Russian publisher", an error), "less war and more peace, no philosophical digressions" (Moscow Times), "half as long and five times as interesting" (Russian Life). Quote none of them as the slogan.
11. **Bromfield draft year:** Wikipedia's section heading "1863 Draft Version" vs Kirkus, Moscow Times and Mail & Guardian "1866". Use 1866.
12. **Clara Bell year:** volume entered for copyright 1885 ("Before Tilsit") and 1886 ("Borodino..."); English Wikipedia gives 1886. Say "1885–86".
13. **Word count of the Maude text:** 562,416 (ours, sibling rules), 561,870 (site, published), 562,677 (scout's token count), 564,088 (Wolfram Data Repository, search summary only). Keep 561,870 or "about 562,000".
14. **Maude edition:** Gutenberg does not say whether #2600 is the 1922–23 text or the 1933 revision. Do not say which.
