# Research: evidence for "How to Learn Spanish With Audiobooks"

Evidence agent, 2026-11-04. Every DOI below was checked against `api.crossref.org/works/<doi>`; titles, journals and years are copied from Crossref unless noted. Abstracts came from OpenAlex, ERIC or Europe PMC. Web pages were fetched on 2026-11-04 and are quoted word for word.

Reused from learn-english-with-audiobooks (the limit was two): **van Zeeland & Schmitt 2013** and **Zhao 1997**. Everything else is new.

## 1. Reading-while-listening, audiobooks and extensive input with non-English L2s

**Main finding: I found no peer-reviewed audiobook or reading-while-listening (RWL) study with adult learners of Spanish.** The closest Spanish evidence covers video with captions (section 2), repeated and slowed listening (section 4), and extensive reading (Rodrigo et al. below). The non-English RWL studies I found were with learners of Japanese and Chinese.

### Peterson (2025 online; 2026 issue): audio-assisted extensive reading, learners of Japanese
- "Exploring general language ability development through monitored extensive reading, extensive listening, and audio‐assisted extensive reading", *Foreign Language Annals* 59(3), 593–615. https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.70043
- Design: experimental. Learners of Japanese as a foreign language were put in monitored extensive reading (ER), extensive listening (EL) or audio-assisted extensive reading (AER) groups for one year and took a standardized test (Japanese Computerized Adaptive Test). The abstract does not give n.
- Result (abstract): "AER outperformed all other groups on the Japanese Computerized Adaptive Test, suggesting that incorporating AER into the curriculum can foster growth in global language competence more effectively than ER or EL alone."
- Shows: across a year, reading along with audio beat reading alone and listening alone on a general proficiency test, in a non-English L2. Does not show: anything about Spanish, effect sizes (not in the abstract), or a large sample (unknown).

### Zhang & Peng (2024): reading-only vs reading-while-listening, learners of Chinese
- "Multimodal reading in reading-only versus reading-while-listening modes: evidence from Chinese language learners", *Chinese as a Second Language Research* 13(2), 215–236. https://doi.org/10.1515/caslar-2024-2003
- Design: within-learner comparison of reading-only and RWL, with intermediate-low and intermediate-high learners. t-tests plus interviews.
- Result: audio "significant[ly]" improved comprehension for **intermediate-low** learners, who also preferred it. There was no significant effect for **intermediate-high** learners, who had "a mixed attitude towards audio integration".
- Shows: audio support helps most at lower levels. Does not show: long-term learning. It is a single-session comprehension test.

### Hui & Godfroid (2025 online; 2026 issue): RWL hurt comprehension compared with silent reading
- "Listening, Reading, or Both? Rethinking the Comprehension Benefits of Reading‐While‐Listening", *Language Learning* 76(1), 311–351. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12721 (open access)
- Design: preregistered registered report, within-participant. **86** intermediate-to-advanced Chinese learners of English read, read-while-listened and listened to excerpts of a novel.
- Result: "Contrary to our preregistered hypotheses, participants comprehended the text less well when reading‐while‐listening than when reading it silently. Both reading conditions yielded better comprehension than listening‐only."
- Shows: for immediate comprehension of a novel, adding audio to text did not help these learners, but text plus audio beat audio alone. Does not show: effects on listening skill, pronunciation or vocabulary over time (it tested comprehension only). The learners were learning English. **Use this for honesty: read-along makes audio easier to follow; it is not proven to beat silent reading for understanding a page.**

### Rodrigo, Krashen & Gribbons (2004): extensive reading in L2 Spanish
- "The effectiveness of two comprehensible-input approaches to foreign language instruction at the intermediate level", *System* 32(1), 53–60. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2003.08.003 (ERIC EJ803951)
- Design: fourth-semester US university students of Spanish in three intact classes: extensive reading (assigned plus self-selected), Reading-Discussion, and a traditional class. ERIC does not give n.
- Result (ERIC): "Students in both classes outperformed those in a traditionally taught class on a check-list vocabulary test and on a grammar test. The Reading-Discussion group outperformed the Traditional students on a cloze test (P=0.105), but there was no difference between Traditional and Reading students on the cloze."
- Shows: input-heavy Spanish classes kept up with or beat a traditional class on vocabulary and grammar. Does not show: anything about audio. The classes were intact (not randomized), and the reported p = .105 is not conventionally significant. Krashen is a co-author and the originator of the input hypothesis.

### Bisson, van Heuven, Conklin & Tunney (2013, 2014): incidental Welsh vocabulary from heard and written words
- 2013: "Incidental Acquisition of Foreign Language Vocabulary through Brief Multi-Modal Exposure", *PLoS ONE* 8(4), e60912. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0060912. **66** native English speakers with no Welsh saw 40 Welsh words, each "the auditory and written forms ... presented simultaneously with a picture", 8 repetitions.
- 2014: "The Role of Repeated Exposure to Multimodal Input in Incidental Acquisition of Foreign Language Vocabulary", *Language Learning* 64(4), 855–877. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12085. Learning occurred "from as little as 2 exposures to the multimodal stimuli", and repetition helped most "during the first few exposures and decrease[d] thereafter".
- Shows: complete beginners pick up some form-meaning links from hearing and seeing a word together, without trying. Does not show: anything at the scale of a book. These were lab tasks with pictures.

### Not found / UNVERIFIED
- No study of graded readers *with audio* for Spanish learners turned up in Crossref, ERIC or OpenAlex searches.
- Lund (1991), "A Comparison of Second Language Listening and Reading Comprehension", *Modern Language Journal* 75(2), 196–204 (https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1991.tb05350.x; L2 German). Crossref verified the metadata, but **no abstract was retrievable, so its findings are UNVERIFIED. Do not cite findings from it.**

## 2. Captions and same-language subtitles

### Montero Perez, Van Den Noortgate & Desmet (2013): meta-analysis
- "Captioned video for L2 listening and vocabulary learning: A meta-analysis", *System* 41(3), 720–739. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2013.07.013
- Design: random-effects meta-analysis, with 15 studies for listening comprehension and 10 for vocabulary (counts from secondary indexes of the abstract).
- Result: a large effect of captions on both listening comprehension and vocabulary. Test type moderated the listening effect; proficiency did not moderate either outcome. The author's 2013 KU Leuven thesis abstract (Lirias) says: "The results of the meta-analyses show overall large effect sizes demonstrating the overall superiority of full captioning for enhancing comprehension and vocabulary learning gains."
- **The exact pooled effect sizes (often quoted as g ≈ 0.99 for listening and g ≈ 0.87 for vocabulary) are UNVERIFIED.** ScienceDirect blocked the fetch with a captcha, and no open copy was found. In the post, write "a large effect" or use Reynolds et al. below for a number.
- Shows: captions help L2 viewers understand and learn words from video. Does not show: audio-only with text (audiobooks), or long-term gains.

### Reynolds, Cui, Kao & Thomas (2022): newer meta-analysis
- "Vocabulary Acquisition through Viewing Captioned and Subtitled Video: A Scoping Review and Meta-Analysis", *Systems* 10(5), 133. https://doi.org/10.3390/systems10050133 (open access)
- Design: 139 studies screened and 34 included in the scoping review. The overall-effect analysis used **k = 20**. More than half of the studies were with L2 English learners (k = 24), **followed by French (k = 5)**.
- Result: "Large effect sizes were found in favor of captioning/subtitling under the fixed-effect model (d = 0.874) and the random-effects model (d = 0.884)." Heterogeneity was very high (I² = 94.8). "Among all the captioned and subtitled video types, viewing videos with intralingual captions had the largest effect".
- Shows: same-language text alongside L2 audio is consistently linked to vocabulary gains. Does not show: Spanish specifically. The study pool is mostly English learners, and the effects vary widely between studies.

### Montero Perez (2022): narrative review
- "Second or foreign language learning through watching audio-visual input and the role of on-screen text", *Language Teaching* 55(2), 163–192. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444821000501
- A state-of-the-art review covering vocabulary demands of video, comprehension with captions and subtitles, and effects on vocabulary, grammar and listening. Use it as the "review of the field" citation; it is not a source of new numbers.

### Montero Perez & Pattemore (2025): captions and speech segmentation in L2 Spanish (the best Spanish-specific study)
- "Effects of captioned video on L2 speech segmentation in intermediate learners of Spanish", *Language Learning & Technology* 29(3), 205–225. https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/73653 (resolves to hdl.handle.net/10125/73653; open access)
- Design: **37** (low-)intermediate learners of Spanish watched a 72-minute episode of a Spanish TV series. Groups: captioned (n = 12), uncaptioned (n = 14) and control (n = 11). Pre/post shadowing task; LexTALE-ESP vocabulary test.
- Result: "watching video contributed to learners' speech segmentation, irrespective of captioning presence." Gains appeared on treatment sentences and sentences from the same series, but not on sentences from an unrelated series. Larger vocabulary predicted better shadowing.
- Shows: one long session of Spanish TV made learners better at hearing word boundaries in that show's speech. Does not show: that captions added anything to segmentation here, or that the gain transfers to other speakers. The sample is very small.

### Winke, Gass & Sydorenko (2010): captions with learners of Spanish, Arabic, Chinese and Russian
- "The effects of captioning videos used for foreign language listening activities", *Language Learning & Technology* 14(1), 65–86. https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/44203 (open access)
- Design: second- and fourth-year US learners of Arabic, Chinese, Spanish and Russian watched three short videos with and without captions in randomized order. Spanish had two extra groups (twice uncaptioned and twice captioned). (n is not in the abstract; I did not verify it.)
- Result: "captioning was more effective than no captioning." Captioning on the first showing was better for aural vocabulary tests. "For Spanish and Russian, captioning first was generally more effective than captioning second." Learners also said they used captions "as a crutch".
- Shows: for Spanish learners, text on the first pass, then listening, is a sensible order. Does not show: long-term retention.

### Winke, Gass & Sydorenko (2013): eye-tracking follow-up
- "Factors Influencing the Use of Captions by Foreign Language Learners: An Eye‐Tracking Study", *Modern Language Journal* 97(1), 254–275. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2013.01432.x
- Fourth-semester English-speaking learners of Arabic, Chinese, Russian and Spanish. "Arabic learners spent more time on captions than learners of Spanish and Russian." Benefits were "constrained by L2 differences, including differences in script".
- Shows: Spanish learners can follow Latin-script captions without them taking over, which is a relative advantage over learners of other scripts. Does not show: learning gains (this is a processing study).

### Markham, Peter & McCarthy (2001); Markham & Peter (2003): Spanish vs English captions for L2 Spanish
- 2001: "The Effects of Native Language vs. Target Language Captions on Foreign Language Students' DVD Video Comprehension", *Foreign Language Annals* 34(5), 439–445. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1944-9720.2001.tb02083.x. **169** fourth-semester university Spanish students in intact groups, with a 7-minute Spanish-audio DVD episode.
- 2003: "The Influence of English Language and Spanish Language Captions on Foreign Language Listening/Reading Comprehension", *Journal of Educational Technology Systems* 31(3), 331–341. https://doi.org/10.2190/bhuh-420b-fe23-ala0. **213** fourth-semester students, with a 20-item Spanish listening test.
- Result (both): "the English captions group performed at a ... higher level than the Spanish captions group, which in turn performed at a ... higher level than the no captions group".
- Shows: Spanish captions beat no captions, but English captions give the most *immediate* comprehension. Does not show: which condition builds listening skill over time. These were single-session comprehension tests.

### Stewart & Pertusa (2004): Spanish captions vs English subtitles on films
- "Gains to Language Learners from Viewing Target Language Closed‐Captioned Films", *Foreign Language Annals* 37(3), 438–442. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1944-9720.2004.tb02701.x
- Intermediate Spanish conversation classes: English subtitles vs Spanish closed captions. "We found slight differences" in vocabulary recognition. Surveys pointed to "possible benefits" of Spanish captions.
- Shows: honest null-ish evidence; the vocabulary advantage of Spanish captions was small. Does not show: a clear win for same-language text.

### Montero Perez, Peters, Clarebout & Desmet (2014): captions and L2 French
- "Effects of captioning on video comprehension and incidental vocabulary learning", *Language Learning & Technology* 18(1), 118–141. https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/44357
- **133** Flemish undergraduates watched three French clips twice: control n = 32, full captions n = 30, keyword captions n = 34, full captions with highlighted keywords n = 37.
- Result: captioning groups beat the control on form recognition and clip association. Only the keyword groups beat the control on meaning recognition. "Captioning did not affect comprehension nor meaning recall."
- Shows: text with audio helps learners recognize word forms; it does not automatically make meanings stick. Does not show: Spanish.

### Montero Perez, Peters & Desmet (2015): eye-tracking, L2 French
- "Enhancing Vocabulary Learning Through Captioned Video: An Eye‐Tracking Study", *Modern Language Journal* 99(2), 308–328. https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12215
- In the full captioning, intentional group, longer fixations on target words were linked to correct recognition. Keyword groups did best on form recognition.
- Shows: looking at a word while hearing it is linked to learning it.

## 3. How fast is spoken Spanish?

### Pellegrino, Coupé & Marsico (2011)
- "A cross-language perspective on speech information rate", *Language* 87(3), 539–558. https://doi.org/10.1353/lan.2011.0057
- Full text read from the LSA-hosted PDF linked in the 2011 LSA press release (http://lsadc.org/info/documents/2011/press-releases/pellegrino-et-al.pdf, retrieved via the Wayback Machine snapshot of 2011-10-14). The text carries the journal page header "545".
- Design: 20 short texts (five sentences each, from the MULTEXT corpus, written in British English and translated) in seven languages. "Several adult speakers (from six to ten, depending on the language)" per language read them aloud. The press release says "about 60 native speakers". Vietnamese is the reference (= 1).
- **Table 1 numbers** (mean, 95% CI):

| Language | Information density per syllable | Syllabic rate (syllables/sec) | Information rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| English | 0.91 (± 0.04) | 6.19 (± 0.16) | 1.08 (± 0.08) |
| Spanish | 0.63 (± 0.02) | 7.82 (± 0.16) | 0.98 (± 0.07) |
| French | 0.74 (± 0.04) | 7.18 (± 0.12) | 0.99 (± 0.09) |
| Italian | 0.72 (± 0.04) | 6.99 (± 0.23) | 0.96 (± 0.10) |
| German | 0.79 (± 0.03) | 5.97 (± 0.19) | 0.90 (± 0.07) |
| Japanese | 0.49 (± 0.02) | 7.84 (± 0.09) | 0.74 (± 0.06) |
| Mandarin | 0.94 (± 0.04) | 5.18 (± 0.15) | 0.94 (± 0.08) |
| Vietnamese | 1 (reference) | 5.22 (± 0.08) | 1 (reference) |

  The PDF text layer shifts the columns. I checked the alignment against the paper's own prose: "Spanish is characterized by a fast rate of low-density syllables, while Mandarin exhibits a 34% slower syllabic rate with syllables 'denser' by a factor of 49%. In the end, their information rates differ only by 4%." That matches 5.18/7.82 = 0.66, 0.94/0.63 = 1.49 and 0.94 vs 0.98. "English (IR_EN = 1.08) shows a higher information rate than Vietnamese."
- **For the post:** Spanish read speech ran at **7.82 syllables per second against 6.19 for English**, about 26% more syllables per second. Each Spanish syllable carried less information (0.63 vs 0.91), so the information rate was similar (0.98 vs 1.08).
- Shows: Spanish sounds fast because more, lighter syllables go by each second; the meaning per second is not faster. Does not show: spontaneous conversation (these were rehearsed read texts) or dialect differences (only a few speakers per language).

### Coupé, Oh, Dediu & Pellegrino (2019)
- "Different languages, similar encoding efficiency: Comparable information rates across the human communicative niche", *Science Advances* 5(9), eaaw2594. https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aaw2594 (open access, PMC6984970)
- Design: **170** native adult speakers of **17** languages, Spanish included, read 15 parallel texts, about 240,000 syllables in total.
- Result: "IR is centered on a mean of 39.15 bits/s with an SD of 5.10 bits/s, while SR is centered on a mean of 6.63 syllables/s, with an SD of 1.15 syllables/s". Individual speakers ranged "from around 4.3 syllables/s ... [to] 9.1 syllables/s". The abstract: "languages encoding similar information rates (~39 bits/s)".
- Shows: across languages, faster syllables carry less information each. Does not show: a Spanish-specific syllable rate in the main text (per-language values are in the supplementary file, which I did not check).

## 4. Does slowing playback help?

### Zhao (1997) [reused from the English post]
- "The Effects of Listeners' Control of Speech Rate on Second Language Comprehension", *Applied Linguistics* 18(1), 49–68. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/18.1.49
- Abstract: "when given control, students' listening comprehension improved and ... improved listening comprehension was achieved by slowing down the speech rate".
- Sample size is not in the abstract (often reported as 15 ESL students: UNVERIFIED). The learners were English learners. It shows learner-controlled slowing helps in-the-moment comprehension; it does not show long-term listening gains.

### Jensen & Vinther (2003): repetition and slowing with learners of Spanish
- "Exact Repetition as Input Enhancement in Second Language Acquisition", *Language Learning* 53(3), 373–428. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9922.00230
- Design: two experiments with **84** upper-intermediate learners of Spanish (Danish university learners; the L1 is from the full paper, not checked). Video of quasi-spontaneous native dialogues. Each utterance was heard three times in three speed patterns: fast-slow-fast, fast-slow-slow and fast-fast-fast. A fourth group was a no-treatment baseline.
- Result: "Comparisons of pretest and posttest scores showed significant effects for all three parameters [comprehension, decoding strategies, linguistic features]. No difference with regard to effect could be established between treatment conditions."
- Shows: **for Spanish learners, hearing the same utterance three times helped, whether or not the middle pass was slowed.** Repetition did the heavy lifting; slowing added nothing measurable. This is the best Spanish-specific support for "replay a sentence" over "just slow everything down". Does not show: any specific setting like 0.75x.

### McBride (2011): training on slow audio
- "The effect of rate of speech and distributed practice on the development of listening comprehension", *Computer Assisted Language Learning* 24(2), 131–154. https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2010.528777
- EFL learners in four groups: fast recordings, slow recordings, some speed choice, and pausing allowed. "The group that was trained on slow materials fared the best, apparently because their bottom-up processing improved." Spacing practice over several days also helped.
- Shows: practising on slower audio can build decoding. Does not show: Spanish, or a specific playback multiplier.

### 0.75x
- **No study found that tests 0.75x playback for language learners.** Present 0.75x as a practical setting, not an evidence-based number.

## 5. Lexical coverage: how many words you need

### van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013) [reused]
- "Lexical Coverage in L1 and L2 Listening Comprehension: The Same or Different from Reading Comprehension?", *Applied Linguistics* 34(4), 457–479. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/ams074
- **36** native and **40** non-native participants. "most native and non-native participants could adequately comprehend the spoken texts with only 90 per cent coverage ... At 95 per cent coverage, non-native participants also demonstrated relatively good comprehension, but with much less variation." That implies "between 2,000 and 3,000 word families for adequate listening comprehension, compared with Nation's (2006) calculation of 6,000–7,000 families based on a 98 per cent figure."
- English only.

### Hu & Nation (2000)
- "Unknown vocabulary density and reading comprehension", *Reading in a Foreign Language* 13(1), 403–430. https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/66973 (ERIC EJ626518)
- **66** university students in New Zealand (n from Kremmel et al. 2023). This is the source of the "98% of words known" figure for unassisted reading of fiction.

### Kremmel, Indrarathne, Kormos & Suzuki (2023): replication
- "Unknown Vocabulary Density and Reading Comprehension: Replicating Hu and Nation (2000)", *Language Learning* 73(4), 1127–1163 (ERIC EJ1403916). https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12622 (open access)
- **104** Sri Lankan adult L2 learners. "The results of the original study could not be fully replicated."
- Use it to hedge: "98%" is a rule of thumb, not a law.

### Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010)
- "Lexical threshold revisited: Lexical text coverage, learners' vocabulary size and reading comprehension", *Reading in a Foreign Language* 22(1), 15–30. https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/66648 (ERIC EJ887873)
- Two thresholds: "an optimal one, which is the knowledge of 8,000 word families yielding the coverage of 98% (including proper nouns) and a minimal one, which is 4,000-5,000 word families resulting in the coverage of 95%". Sample size not checked.

### Nation (2006)
- "How Large a Vocabulary is Needed For Reading and Listening?", *Canadian Modern Language Review* 63(1), 59–82. https://doi.org/10.3138/cmlr.63.1.59
- "If 98% coverage of a text is needed for unassisted comprehension, then a 8,000 to 9,000 word-family vocabulary is needed for comprehension of written text and a vocabulary of 6,000 to 7,000 for spoken text."
- Novels: the 8,000–9,000 figure is the written-text answer. **The often-quoted single-novel example ("Lady Chatterley's Lover needs about 9,000 families") is UNVERIFIED**, because the full text was not accessible.

### Davies (2005): Spanish-specific coverage (best Spanish number)
- Mark Davies, "Vocabulary Range and Text Coverage: Insights from the Forthcoming Routledge Frequency Dictionary of Spanish", in *Selected Proceedings of the 7th Hispanic Linguistics Symposium*, ed. David Eddington, 106–115. Cascadilla Proceedings Project. https://www.lingref.com/cpp/hls/7/paper1091.pdf (document #1091). No DOI.
- Corpus: similar to the 1900s sub-corpus of the 100-million-word Corpus del Español. Counts are by **lemma** (not word family).
- Table 3 (percent of tokens covered):

| | Non-fiction | Fiction | Oral |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st 1,000 lemmas | 76.0 | 79.6 | 87.8 |
| 2nd 1,000 | 8.0 | 6.5 | 4.9 |
| 3rd 1,000 | 4.2 | 3.5 | 2.3 |
| **First 3,000** | **88.2** | **89.6** | **94.0** |

- Quotes: "a limited vocabulary of 1000 words would allow language learners to recognize between 75-80% of all lexemes in written Spanish, and about 88% of all lexemes in spoken Spanish". "a frequency list with about 4000 words total would cover about 90% of all words that would be heard in a typical conversation". "one would need about 7000 words to achieve 90% coverage in fiction writing, and nearly 8000 words for non-fiction."
- Shows: spoken Spanish is lexically easier than written Spanish; the first 1,000 words cover far more of a conversation than of a novel. Does not show: comprehension (these are coverage counts). Lemma counts are not directly comparable to English word-family thresholds.
- The dictionary itself: Davies & Davies, *A Frequency Dictionary of Spanish*, Routledge (2nd ed. 2017, https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315542638; 1st ed. 2006, https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203415009).
- **Arithmetic for the post (label as an estimate):** fiction needs about 7,000 lemmas for just 90% coverage, well below the 95–98% thresholds above. That is why a beginner should start with graded readers, and why text next to the audio helps.

## 6. FSI: how long Spanish takes

- Source: US Department of State, "Foreign Language Training" (NFATC). https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training/ redirects to https://www.state.gov/national-foreign-affairs-training-center/foreign-language-training. state.gov returns 403 to curl; the page was read on 2026-11-04 through the r.jina.ai reader. The Wayback snapshots also return a block page.
- Exact quotes:
  - "The following language learning timelines indicate the time usually expect ed for a student to reach a n integrated score of 3 (Speaking + Listening) on the Interagency Language Roundtable (ILR) scale, though the actual time can vary based on several factors, including the language learner's natural ability, prior language-learning experience, and time spent in the classroom. A typical week is 23 hours per week in class and 17 hours of self-study." (The stray spaces in "expect ed" and "a n" are on the page.)
  - "**Category I Languages:** 24-30 weeks (552-690 class hours)" / "Languages close to English."
  - The list includes "Spanish (30 weeks)" and "French (30 weeks)"; Danish, Dutch, Italian, Norwegian, Portuguese, Romanian and Swedish are 24 weeks.
- **The page changed.** Older versions said "24-30 weeks (600-750 class hours)" (the UNT web archive copy from Jan 2021 is cited by third-party sites). Use **30 weeks / 690 class hours** for Spanish. Arithmetic: 30 × 23 = 690 class hours, plus 30 × 17 = 510 self-study hours, so about 1,200 hours in total (label as derived).
- Shows: FSI's expectation for full-time, classroom-trained adults to reach ILR 3 (professional working proficiency). Does not show: hobby-learner timelines, or anything about audiobooks.

## 8. Honest limits

### Listening and reading alone do not fully build production
- **Lightbown, Halter, White & Horst (2002)**, "Comprehension-Based Learning: The Limits of 'Do It Yourself'", *Canadian Modern Language Review* 58(3), 427–464. https://doi.org/10.3138/cmlr.58.3.427. Children in a comprehension-only ESL program (listen and read, no speaking instruction) were followed to grade 8. "After six years of an essentially comprehension-based program in ESL, they performed as well as comparison groups of students on measures of comprehension and some measures of oral production but not on measures of written production." The children were French-speaking learners of English (setting from the literature; not re-checked in the full text).
- **Trofimovich, Lightbown, Halter & Song (2009)**, *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 31(4), 609–639. https://doi.org/10.1017/s0272263109990040. Crossref gives the title only as "COMPREHENSION-BASED PRACTICE"; the full published title is "Comprehension-based practice: The development of L2 pronunciation in a listening and reading program". Two-year longitudinal study of grade 3–4 ESL learners, "in the virtual absence of any speaking". There were no differences after year 1, but after year 2 listener ratings of "fluency and comprehensibility" "favored learners in the regular program". It points to "potential limits of this practice".
- **Shintani, Li & Ellis (2013)**, "Comprehension‐Based Versus Production‐Based Grammar Instruction: A Meta‐Analysis of Comparative Studies", *Language Learning* 63(2), 296–329. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12001. 35 projects in 30 studies. Both approaches had large effects. But "for productive knowledge, CBI and PBI had similar effects in short‐term measurement but PBI was more effective in the delayed tests".
- Takeaway for the post: audiobooks build comprehension (and some pronunciation and oral skill), but speaking and writing need practice of their own.

### The input hypothesis is a theory
- Krashen, S. (1982), *Principles and Practice in Second Language Acquisition*, Pergamon. Free PDF from the author: https://www.sdkrashen.com/content/books/principles_and_practice.pdf (HTTP 200 checked). Call it "Krashen's input hypothesis, a theory".
- Classic critique: Gregg, K. R. (1984), "Krashen's Monitor and Occam's Razor", *Applied Linguistics* 5(2), 79–100. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/5.2.79. (Crossref's record has the typo "Occam's Razar".)

### Null or negative results to cite
- RWL comprehended a novel *worse* than silent reading (Hui & Godfroid 2025, n = 86).
- Captioning did not affect comprehension or meaning recall (Montero Perez et al. 2014, n = 133, L2 French).
- Captions did not add to speech-segmentation gains in Spanish (Montero Perez & Pattemore 2025, n = 37).
- Only "slight differences" between Spanish captions and English subtitles for vocabulary (Stewart & Pertusa 2004).
- English (L1) captions beat Spanish captions for immediate comprehension (Markham et al. 2001, n = 169; Markham & Peter 2003, n = 213).
- The 98% threshold "could not be fully replicated" (Kremmel et al. 2023, n = 104).
- Slowing the middle replay added nothing over plain repetition (Jensen & Vinther 2003, n = 84, L2 Spanish).

### "Dreaming Spanish" hour claims are vendor claims
- Dreaming FAQ, https://www.dreaming.com/faq (fetched 2026-11-04):
  - "What are the 7 levels and the number of hours for each one based on? The levels in our roadmap were initially based on the experiences of thousands of students that learned Thai with comprehensible input at AUA, and contrasted with Pablo's experience learning languages that are related to his first language, and languages that are completely different. Since creating our roadmap, we have received tons of feedback from students that have repeatedly reported being surprised at the accuracy of the length and the descriptions of the different levels."
  - "Should I ever practice speaking? When? Definitely! We recommend doing so once you get to level 6 in the language learning roadmap."
  - The FAQ cites "Dr. Stephen Krashen" as the scientific basis.
- The per-level hour thresholds (commonly 50 / 150 / 300 / 600 / 1,000 / 1,500 hours, with level 6 = 1,000 h) are on a progress page that requires login; I saw them only in community sources. **UNVERIFIED as official.** In the post, call them "a course's roadmap, built from its founders' and students' experience, not from a controlled study."

## 9. Accent variety (Castilian vs Latin American)

### Schoonmaker-Gates (2018)
- "Dialect comprehension and identification in L2 Spanish: Familiarity and type of exposure", *Studies in Hispanic and Lusophone Linguistics* 11(1), 193–214. https://doi.org/10.1515/shll-2018-0007
- **60** L1 English learners of Spanish heard read speech from **six** regional varieties, with transcription (comprehension) and identification tasks.
- Result: "familiarity was a significant predictor of learners' dialect identification regardless of level, and it also predicted the dialect comprehension of more advanced students. Comprehension but not identification was also more accurate when multiple types of exposure were reported and when exposure was through native instructors, study abroad, and media from a specific region."
- Shows: learners understand the accents they have heard more of, and listening to media from a region helps. Does not show: that any one variety (Castilian or Latin American) is easier as a starting point. **No study found that ranks Castilian vs Latin American Spanish for learner comprehension.**
- Practical line for the post: pick the accent you are most likely to need, and add other narrators once that one feels easy.

## What the evidence does not show

1. No controlled study of adults learning **Spanish** from **audiobooks** or reading-while-listening was found. The Spanish evidence comes from video with captions, repeated listening and extensive reading. RWL evidence for non-English L2s is limited to Japanese (Peterson) and Chinese (Zhang & Peng).
2. That reading along beats silent reading for comprehension. A 2025 registered report found the opposite for a novel (Hui & Godfroid). The evidence favours text + audio over **audio alone**.
3. That any particular playback speed (0.75x or another) is optimal. Learner-controlled slowing helps comprehension in the moment (Zhao); repetition helped Spanish learners whether or not it was slowed (Jensen & Vinther).
4. That listening builds speaking or writing on its own. Comprehension-only programs fell behind on written production (Lightbown et al.) and later on fluency and comprehensibility ratings (Trofimovich et al.).
5. A verified number of hours to "fluency" from listening. Dreaming-style hour thresholds are vendor roadmaps; FSI's 30 weeks / 690 class hours applies to full-time classroom training to ILR 3.
6. That Spanish carries more meaning per second than English. It packs more syllables per second (7.82 vs 6.19) with less information per syllable (Pellegrino et al.), and those figures come from read speech by 6–10 speakers per language.
7. That one Spanish accent is easier to learn from. Only familiarity effects are shown (Schoonmaker-Gates).
8. The exact pooled effect sizes in Montero Perez et al. (2013) (UNVERIFIED). Use "large" or Reynolds et al.'s d = 0.88.
